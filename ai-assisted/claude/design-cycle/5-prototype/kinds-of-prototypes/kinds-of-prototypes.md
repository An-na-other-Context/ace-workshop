> **AI-made.** Drafted by Claude (AI) from Anna (@annaothercrow)'s solo design-thinking toolkit, which adapts *The Design Thinking Toolbox* and *The Design Thinking Playbook* for one person. The choices are Anna's. See [Who made what](#who-made-what) and [Sources](#sources).

## What it is

This one is a map. It has no steps and no sheet to fill in. It shows the seven kinds of prototype the book names, in order of growing detail, so you can look up what to build when (Toolbox p. 187).

- **Resolution** means how detailed and finished a prototype is. Low resolution is rough and quick, made from simple materials. High resolution is detailed and close to the finished thing.
- **Which kind fits** depends on the problem. So do how many prototypes you build and how often you loop back (p. 187).
- **Detail usually grows** from low resolution to high. The first ideas are often just sketches (p. 187).
- **Several of one kind** are normal, and you can move from any kind to any other at any time. The [exploration map](../exploration-map/exploration-map.md) keeps track of where you've been (p. 187).
- **In the cycle,** each Prototype tool does its own job: this page is the map, [Prototype to test](../prototype-to-test/prototype-to-test.md) is the doing, the [exploration map](../exploration-map/exploration-map.md) is the record, and the [MVP](../mvp/mvp.md) (minimum viable product) is the far end, used rarely (Anna, 20.09.2026).
- **Optional:** look it up when a run needs it, and skip it when it doesn't (Anna, 20.09.2026).

## The seven kinds, in order of detail (Toolbox p. 187–194)

Each kind as the book describes it, in other words. The lines marked "Alone" are Claude's.

**1 · Critical experience or critical function** (p. 188). Small experiments, made early while Understand and Observe are still fresh. Each one questions a single element of a possible solution, without trying to solve the whole problem. They reach needs and feelings that an interview tends to miss. The [critical items diagram](../../3-define/critical-items-diagram/critical-items-diagram.md) is their base. Alone: each of its eight elements can become one small experiment.

**2 · Dark horse** (p. 189). The unexpected winner. Deliberately unusual experiments that test how people react to odd approaches: asking what would happen if, or picturing the solution thirty years from now. Risky ideas belong here, and failing is "allowed, if not encouraged" (p. 189, quoted). A Wizard of Oz setup is the usual way to fake what can't be built yet: a hidden person plays the part of the system. Alone: the hidden person can be Claude.

**3 · Funky** (p. 190). Brings what the earlier experiments found (the critical ones, the dark horse, brainstorms) together into one rough whole, to clear up what is still uncertain. The first real functions appear, made from simple materials or from things that already exist. Alone: made from parts you already have, such as sheets, pages and cards.

**4 · Vision** (p. 191). The first concept that tries to meet all the needs at once, aimed at a far horizon. It carries the team across the "groan zone" (p. 191, quoted): the turn from exploring the problem to solving it. One sentence should describe the state you are aiming for. If it sparks interest, the path looks promising. Still low resolution: a sketch or a video is enough.

**5 · Functional (system)** (p. 192). One part of the vision that really works, usually the main function. It is kept as simple as possible and checked for whether it can be built. This is where an MVP can first appear. The book is open about the hurdle: the step into technical building takes courage, because failing is likely. Its answer: "There is no failure, there's only learning" (p. 192, quoted).

**6 · "X is finished"** (p. 193). The details: the sub-functions the whole thing needs, each specified as far as it can be, and checked for whether people can really use them. First estimates of effort and cost appear. Alone: only worth reaching when a run depends on it.

**7 · Final** (p. 194). The finishing touches, plus a check that the solution still meets the needs you started from, because detail work makes it easy to lose the big picture. At the latest here, the MVP is reached. Alone: the point where something stops being an experiment and becomes part of what you are making.

## The book, and the version for one person

The overview has no group size or time of its own. These four rows are what changes when nobody else is in the room.

| | The book (Toolbox p. 187–194) | Alone |
|---|---|---|
| Who tests it | Users. Every kind is made to be handed to someone and watched. The critical kind exists to reach feelings a questionnaire can't (p. 188). | You, first. A prototype you use yourself still answers *does this work for me?* Other people can come later, if you want them. |
| How long | Short. Experiments should be made very quickly, and many small ones usually teach more than one big one (p. 188, 190). | No fixed time (Anna, 19.09.2026). Small things tend to finish while the energy is still there. |
| Resolution | From low to high. Detail grows over time, and the first ideas are usually sketches. Several of one kind each time, and any kind can lead to any other (p. 187). | Paper, a page, grey boxes. Code earns its place at the functional kind. |
| Failure | At the dark horse, failing is welcome. At the functional kind, the book says there is only learning (p. 189, 192). | Iterate often, and never "I failed" (Anna, 18.09.2026). The book says the same in its own words. |

"Alone" lines: Claude's suggestions, unless marked as Anna's.

## The four checks, and where they come from

Claude proposed these on the toolkit page, which Anna kept on 20.09.2026. You can glance at them when you pick a kind in [Prototype to test](../prototype-to-test/prototype-to-test.md).

| Check | What the book says |
|---|---|
| 1 · The kind is named before building | Which prototype to use depends on the context of the problem statement (p. 187). Naming it first tells you what the thing has to answer. |
| 2 · It stays low resolution while the question is open | Detail usually grows from low to high, and early ideas are mostly simple sketches (p. 187). |
| 3 · Several small ones | Many small experiments often bring more findings than one big one (p. 188). |
| 4 · A failed one counts as learning | At the dark horse, failing is allowed and even encouraged; at the functional kind there is only learning (p. 189, 192). Anna's verdict says the same (18.09.2026). A failed prototype gets written down and kept. |
