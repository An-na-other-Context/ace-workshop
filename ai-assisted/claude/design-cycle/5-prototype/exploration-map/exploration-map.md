## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 195–197):

- **Goal:** know which experiments you have run, and be able to place them.
- **What it is:** a map with two axes. Across: how new the behaviour is, from familiar to a whole new behaviour. Up: how new the function is, from available and doable to unknown, new functions or technologies. Four zones sit on it, from the inside out: comfort, stretch, risk and dark. The current situation is marked, and a goal is drawn further out.
- **What else goes on it:** feedback from tests, so you can see whether the behaviour you expected matches what happened.
- **Use it to:** see which experiments ran and which prototypes exist · see what could still be tried · record the gap between expected and actual results · see, at the end of a cycle, the path you took.
- **Without it:** experiments can be written up the usual way. Then it stays unclear whether anything risky or unusual was tried, and how far you dared to go.
- **Why the book's expert picks it:** teams find it hard to tell whether they have really explored the whole problem space. The map shows that simply, along with what each step taught.
- **In the cycle,** this is the record of phase 5 (Anna, 20.09.2026). It is the one tool that carries across iterations, and it is never finished.

## The cycle, alone

Added to in short visits, one after each experiment.

1. **Enter what you did.** Each experiment gets a name and a picture. Its place may move later. (book step 1)
2. **Read the empty zones.** Have I left the comfort zone lately? The empty zone suggests the next experiment. (book step 2)
3. **Write the expectation** for the next experiment, before its test. It stays on the map even when the result moves the experiment. (book step 3, and an expert tip)
4. **Add what happened:** the reaction, and how far it landed from the expectation. (book step 4)

After every experiment → back to 1. The clarity check shows what is missing; not yet passing just means something to fill in on the next visit.

No matrix walk here: the empty zones do the choosing. No fixed time (Anna, 19.09.2026), and the placing stays quick, because the map is a rough guide (p. 197).

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 197–198)

- **Add to it after every round** (p. 197), so others can read the results. Alone: after every experiment. The reader is you next week.
- **Keep the first expectation when an experiment moves** (p. 197). The distance between expectation and reaction is what you learned, so both marks stay.
- **Two maps side by side** (p. 197), one for expectations and one for reactions. Alone: only if one map gets crowded.
- **Hang it where it's seen** (p. 197), as a big poster. Alone: pin it.
- **Rough orientation** (p. 197). The map is no precise measuring tool, so the placing stays quick. A guard against perfectionism, built into the tool.
- **Use pictures** (p. 197) of the built prototypes. Alone: a screenshot per experiment is enough.
- **The use case** (p. 198): once the team understood the problem, they wanted to pull users out of their comfort zone and try bolder approaches, with a dark horse prototype to enter the dark zone. Alone: the map shows how long everything has stayed in the comfort zone. The dark horse (see [Kinds of prototypes](../kinds-of-prototypes/kinds-of-prototypes.md)) belongs in the dark zone.
- **Key learnings** (p. 198): it keeps track of the experiments and shows where you are on the way · it shows what an experiment set out to learn, and how results change over time. Alone: the same, across iterations.
