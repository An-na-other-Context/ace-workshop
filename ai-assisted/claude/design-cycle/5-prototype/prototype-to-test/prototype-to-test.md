## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 199–201):

- **Goal:** find out whether the ideas you built really meet the need.
- **What it is:** the core move of design thinking. Ideas become prototypes, and people try them. The prototype is set up as an experiment, so you learn something about one feature or one experience (p. 199).
- **The template asks four things:** why (which assumptions to check) · how the person gets to experience it · what to build (the possible variants) · which one to choose, and how the experiment runs.
- **Use it to:** let someone experience an idea and watch how they use it · understand the person better · check needs and assumptions · learn whether it is wanted, workable and practical.
- **Resolution** (how detailed and finished a prototype is): low resolution answers questions about needs, practicality and function, early on. High resolution is about whether it can be built and what it costs (p. 200).
- **Why the book's expert picks it:** it makes even wild ideas, a dark horse for example, concrete enough for a person to grasp. It tests a need in a setting you couldn't reach otherwise. And building is fun.
- **In the cycle,** this is the doing of phase 5 (Anna, 20.09.2026). [Kinds of prototypes](../kinds-of-prototypes/kinds-of-prototypes.md) is the map to look things up in, and the [exploration map](../exploration-map/exploration-map.md) keeps the record afterwards.

## The cycle, alone

One question per prototype, at the lowest resolution that could answer it.

1. **The assumption.** One sentence, written before anything is built: what do I want to learn, and why an experiment at all? (book step 1)
2. **Variants.** Two or three, each at the lowest resolution that could answer the question. [Kinds of prototypes](../kinds-of-prototypes/kinds-of-prototypes.md) can help you pick a form. (book steps 2–3, and an expert tip)
3. **Choose and outline.** Walk the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) over the variants, out loud. The one that goes forward gets an outline: how the run works, and what you expect. (book step 4)
4. **Clarity check.** All five pass → build it, use it in a real run, then put it on the [exploration map](../exploration-map/exploration-map.md) with the expectation and the result. Not yet → back to 1. If two questions hide in one prototype, split them and plan each one on its own.

A real run is a real session: an item on the [ACE Workshop board](https://github.com/orgs/An-na-other-Context/projects/1), logged on its issue. A demo can't answer the question.

No fixed time, and stopping is always allowed (Anna, 19.09.2026).

## The clarity checks, and where they come from

| Check | What the book says |
|---|---|
| 1 · Assumption first | Before building, write down the assumptions to check and how the experiment will run (p. 200). |
| 2 · Two variants or more | Define several prototypes. It often pays to think in alternatives and then pick one (p. 200). |
| 3 · Lowest resolution that can answer it | The lower the resolution, the more honest the answer. Don't put in too much time (p. 201). |
| 4 · One question per prototype | Put no more than one or two functions into a low-resolution prototype (p. 201). Alone, one question each. (Claude's wording) |
| 5 · Clear to me tomorrow | Anna's own check for Problem statement and Jobs to be done (Anna, 19.09.2026). Alone, the reader is you on another day. |

## The book, and the version for one person

| | The book (p. 199–202) | Alone |
|---|---|---|
| People | One person or a larger team can build a prototype (p. 200). Experts and extra team members help check that nothing was missed. The test itself needs a user. | The book allows one builder. You are the first user. Others can come later, and Claude can read the plan back before you build. |
| Time | 30 minutes to many days. A low-resolution prototype can take half an hour; a final one takes days or weeks (p. 200). Too much time raises the resolution and piles on functions (p. 201). | No fixed time (Anna, 19.09.2026). One sitting is a good size. If it needs more, the resolution may be higher than the question needs. |
| Materials | Paper, sticky notes, pens, whatever the prototype needs. Simple on purpose: the lower the resolution, the more honest the feedback (p. 200–201). | Paper, a card, a page, grey boxes. Code only when the question is about a function. |
| Choosing | Define several prototypes, think in alternatives, then pick one and outline the experiment (p. 200). | Two or three variants, then the discernment matrix (Anna, 20.09.2026). |
| What a test is for | An experiment where the person uses the prototype and experiences it. The feedback guides the next decisions (p. 199). | A real run on an ordinary day. It should hold up on a hard day, too. |

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 201–202)

- **The lower the resolution, the more honest the answer** (p. 201). High resolution brings less feedback about whether people want it and whether it works. Alone: paper before pixels. A card you can cross out is quicker to change than a page you'd rebuild.
- **Don't fall in love with your prototype** (p. 201). People hold on to what they built, even when a better idea exists. Keep looking after good feedback, and ask whether it was honest. Alone, this pull is your own; the matrix and the parking lot help against it.
- **Keep the time small** (p. 201). Too much time raises the resolution and loads the prototype with functions: one or two functions per low-resolution prototype. Alone: one question per prototype.
- **Test while it still looks rough** (p. 201). Read it as a size rule: if it already looks finished, more time went in than the question needed. It says nothing about you.
- **Give the context, and keep it apart from the test** (p. 201). Show the prototype instead of explaining it. Alone: write the context down before you start, then let the prototype speak without coaching yourself through it.
- **The use case** (p. 202): make sure the right assumptions get tested, learn as much as possible quickly, with several rounds if needed, think in variants before building, and judge the prototype only after testing. Alone: the assumption first, two or three variants on paper, then build one.
- **Key learnings** (p. 202): show instead of telling · don't fall in love with a prototype · build at the lowest resolution and test even when it feels rough · many small experiments teach more than one big solution nobody wants. Alone: small and often.

"Alone" lines: Claude's suggestions, unless marked as Anna's.
