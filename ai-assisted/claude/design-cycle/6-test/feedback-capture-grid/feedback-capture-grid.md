## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 217, 219):

- **Goal:** test prototyped ideas quickly and simply, and write the results down so they can be used.
- **What it is:** four fields on one sheet. Top left: what the person liked. Top right: what they didn't. Bottom left: the questions that came up. Bottom right: ideas, theirs and yours while watching.
- **What it finds out:** how well an idea solves a problem you already found, and whether it's the right kind of answer at all.
- **Use it to:** test early prototypes with four set questions · collect and cluster results · sharpen what you believe about problems, solutions and people · take quick, ordered notes on anything, a workshop or a process included.
- **Why the expert picks it** (Katrin Fischer): she kept seeing costly projects that tested only at the end. The grid gives test results a simple structure the work can carry on from.

## The cycle, alone

One grid per run.

1. **Run, don't narrate.** Use the thing. Don't explain it to yourself; let the recording watch.
2. **Fill the four fields** right after, from the recording: liked · wished · questions · ideas, in your own words.
3. **Ask one why** about what stands out most, then stop: enough to reach the reason, never enough to hurt. This is Anna's [gentle why](../../supports/gentle-why/gentle-why.md).
4. **Compare grids.** Two or three runs: what's the same, what's different, and a new point of view. Then the clarity check. All five pass → the ideas go to a [brainstorming](../../4-ideate/brainstorming/brainstorming.md) round, and the point of view goes to the [problem statement](../../1-understand/problem-statement/problem-statement.md). Not yet, with only one grid → one grid is an anecdote: run it again on a different kind of day, from 1.

The book's six steps (p. 218): draw the grid · let the person meet the prototype · ask them to think aloud · fill the four fields · ask why · compare. Here, its steps 2 and 3 are 1, its step 4 is 2, its step 5 is 3, its step 6 is 4.

No matrix walk here: this tool captures, and nothing gets chosen. You can stop after any step; the bookmark holds your place.

This tool captures and doesn't choose, so the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) isn't walked here. It sorts the ideas later, in brainstorming.

- [ ] 4 · Two or more grids compared

## The book, and the version for one person

| | The book (p. 217–220) | Alone |
|---|---|---|
| People | at least 2: one interviews, watches and shows the prototype; the other documents and asks follow-up questions. The key learning is to always interview in pairs (p. 218, 220). | The honest gap: someone has to watch while someone else uses the thing. Alone, a recording watches, and the grid gets filled right after the run instead of during it. |

| Materials | the prototype or MVP (minimum viable product), and one printed or drawn grid per tester and interview; A3 works best (p. 218) | One grid per run, in this file or on the board. Keep them all; they get compared later. |

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 219–220)

- **Test in different places and with different user groups.** People who aren't customers yet are especially valuable. Alone: different days and states, such as a good day, a flat one and an overloaded one.
- **Never sell the idea.** Listen and watch, and hold back questions and explanations at first. Alone: no defending it to yourself.
- **Write down direct quotes and first reactions.** Alone: your own words, in the moment, spelling and all.
- **Testing is need finding** (finding out what people need), with the same rules as an interview. Alone: the grid belongs to Observe as much as to Test.
- **Step back afterwards and reflect.** A new point of view often comes out of it. Grouping grids by answers and personas (made-up profiles of typical users) helps. Alone: group your grids by the kind of day.
- **Keep going round again** (iterate) until the people it's for are happy. Alone: until a run feels like something you'd open again.
- **The use case** (p. 220): Lilly's team uses the grid in almost every phase, mostly to document and sum up tests with users. They also use it to review an iteration in the retrospective. Lilly values direct, open feedback and helpful criticism, because it improves both the content and the process. Alone: the grid reaches far beyond Test. In Reflect it can look back at a whole run.
- **Key learnings** (p. 220): always interview in pairs · don't sell the idea; listen with curiosity, watch and learn · talk the findings through and form a new shared point of view. Alone: the pair is the part that doesn't work alone, and a recording isn't the same · the same · write the new point of view down, or talk it through with Claude.

## Parked: the second person (p. 218–220)

The book always puts two people in the interview: one shows the prototype and watches, the other documents and asks follow-up questions. It tests in different places, with different user groups, and values people who aren't customers yet. Alone, there's no second person and no user group. A recording stands in for the second person, and the kind of day stands in for the user group; neither is the same thing. Both parts wait for the day someone else tests. The lean canvas (p. 251), a market tool the book names, waits with them.
