## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 213, 215):

- **Goal:** get a test ready, and write down what it brought.
- **Three parts:** the plan (place, criteria, sequence, roles, key questions) · the run (watch closely, ask for feedback, note the quotes) · the documentation (photos or short videos, then the main findings).
- **Why plan it:** to learn as much as possible in little time, and to see whether your assumptions and hypotheses hold.
- **Use it to:** plan a test step by step and settle who does what · document it so the results help next time · decide beforehand what counts as verified · build empathy for the person using the thing.
- **Why the expert picks it** (Isabelle Hauser, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts): she keeps the early phases minimal, and a well-planned test makes the most of the time and material at hand.

## The cycle, alone

1. **Plan before the day.** Where, what counts as verified, the sequence, the questions, what records the run.
2. **Run it for real.** A real session. If the prototype wants changing, change it on the spot.
3. **Catch it in the moment.** Quotes in your own words, what you changed, what surprised you.
4. **Three findings.** Sum them up in a few lines, then the clarity check and the verdict. Criteria met → the findings go on to the [feedback capture grid](../../6-test/feedback-capture-grid/feedback-capture-grid.md) and the [exploration map](../../5-prototype/exploration-map/exploration-map.md). Not met → change one thing and run again, from 2.

The book's three steps (p. 214) sit inside these four: its plan is 1, its run is 2 and 3, its documentation is 4.

No matrix walk here. The criteria you write before the test do that job: they keep the verdict from being settled afterwards by feel. You can stop after any step; the bookmark holds your place.

## The book, and the version for one person

| | The book (p. 214–216) | Alone |
|---|---|---|
| People | 2–3: one runs the test, one takes notes and documents, a third may observe. Roles can swap when someone gets on better with the test person. | The roles fold into you. A recording takes the observer's place: a voice memo, or the screen. The gap stays real: you can't watch yourself closely while doing the thing. |
| Time | 10–30 minutes per test for low-resolution prototypes. More detail takes longer, and high-resolution tests can run for weeks. The expert: enough time is the most important part of planning (p. 215). | The test happens inside a run: an item on the [ACE Workshop board](https://github.com/orgs/An-na-other-Context/projects/1), logged on its issue. ## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 215–216)

- **One sheet as the base for any test:** usability tests, experience tests, solution interviews. Alone: one sheet for every test you run, whatever its shape.
- **Enough time is the most important part of planning.** Alone: plan the run before the day, so the day only holds the run.
- **Prepare questions that serve the test's goal.** The book points to powerful questions. Alone: take three or four from that bank, for your prototype's resolution.
- **Bring prototyping material.** With tape and sticky notes, the prototype can change on the spot. Alone: change it while you're in it, then note what you changed.
- **When it isn't working, listen more closely and find out why,** asking why several times. Alone: Anna's version, the [gentle why](../../supports/gentle-why/gentle-why.md), stops at the first sign of discomfort. That stop rule comes from her no list, the things she has ruled out (Anna, 18.09.2026).
- **Ask before taking photos or videos,** and get a data use agreement signed for further use. Alone: not needed while you test yourself. It matters the day someone else tests.
- **The use case** (p. 216): the better a test is planned and run, the better the findings. Lilly's team tests in context, at the users' place where it can, with criteria, sequence and roles set beforehand. Alone: a real session on a real day. Criteria and sequence beforehand, and every role is yours.
- **Key learnings** (p. 216): tests give feedback on the prototype and on the user · plan the situation, its sequence and who plays which role · document so results can be shared. Alone: feedback on the thing and on you · the sequence and what records it · documented so the exploration map and the next run can use it.

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **Structured usability testing** (p. 229), an alternative: real users do set tasks under the same conditions. Its observer half needs other people, and Anna left it out of her toolkit. Not built here.
- **Solution interview** (p. 225), an alternative: a 30-minute interview about a nearly finished solution. It needs another person by design. Not built here.
- **Powerful questions in experience testing** (p. 221–224), a helper: a bank of questions sorted by resolution. Step 1 takes three or four from it. Not built here.
- **[Feedback capture grid](../../6-test/feedback-capture-grid/feedback-capture-grid.md)** (p. 217), a helper: what you fill in during or right after the run. Plan here, capture there.
- **Empathy map** (p. 93), a helper: what a person sees, hears, thinks and feels, says and does. Anna didn't choose it (19.09.2026). Not built here.

## Parked: the other people in a test (p. 214–216)

The book plans who asks, who writes and who observes, and swaps roles when someone gets on better with the test person. It asks permission before photos or videos, and has a data use agreement signed for further use. Alone, every role is yours and a recording stands in for the observer. These parts wait for the day someone else tests a prototype. Two tests built around other people wait with them: the solution interview (p. 225) and structured usability testing (p. 229).
