## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 137–139):

- **Goal:** support the whole cycle by saying what success actually means, especially when there are many options.
- **What it is:** a set of questions, answered on different timelines. The questions stay much the same; the horizon moves, for example to a month from now, or to five years.
- **What it produces:** milestones, for solving the problem and for putting the solution into practice.
- **Use it to:** agree on what success is · make sure everyone's requirements are understood · simplify the list of options, and their order, through the whole project · lay a base for measures, if you want them.
- **Why the book's expert uses it** (Helene Cahen, Strategic Insights): it clears away assumptions at the start and brings everyone to one picture of success. Regular reflection shows changes early, so effort can move where it's needed.

## The cycle, alone

1. **Ask on at least two timelines:** this run, a month, a year. The book's questions, rewritten for one person.
2. **Answer without judging.** Write everything down first. Commenting on your answers can wait.
3. **Narrow with the matrix.** Cluster the answers, then walk the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) out loud over them, in place of the book's vote.
4. **Milestone, then the clarity check.** Write the definition and one first milestone you can check. Then check. All five pass → done. Not yet → one more round of answers, then narrow again.

No fixed time (Anna, 19.09.2026). Choosing gets about twice as long as collecting, as the book's expert suggests (p. 139).

Cluster the answers first. Then ask each one these four questions, from the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md):

2. Would reaching it add value right now? → goes forward if: yes
3. Can I tell I reached it, with what I have? → goes forward if: doable
4. Does it get costlier if I leave it undefined? → goes forward if: costlier

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 139–140)

- **Everybody writes their answers down**, so a neutral view of the wishes emerges. Alone: write before you judge, so the first answer doesn't steer the rest.
- **When an answer is shared, just listen and put it up**, with no comment yet. Alone: collect every answer first; judging gets its own step. That keeps perfectionism out of the flow.
- **Plan enough time for assessing and selecting**, as a rule twice as much as for collecting. Alone: the same ratio, in cycles.
- **Look for known success stories**, inside and outside the organisation, to sense what is possible. Alone: your own, such as the discernment matrix that worked in its live test (21.07.2026), and the tools you've kept.
- **The use case** (p. 140): Lilly's team defines early what success means, and which parts must be there for everyone to call it a great project. Alone: what has to be true for you to say it was a good run? The team found that the collaboration and the benefit for society mattered too, besides the economic side, and gives the social and emotional side more attention in the next rounds. Alone: that is clarity check 3, a success that isn't output.
- **Key learnings** (p. 140): define success as early as possible (alone: before the first prototype) · invite the key people, team members and decision makers (alone: that's you, and Claude can ask back if you'd like a second voice) · new information and outside influences mean the definition needs reflecting on again and again (alone: look at it again in Reflect; it may change).

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **Storytelling** (p. 129), an alternative: interprets a vision through users, quotes, meaning and a conclusion. Not built here.
- **Design principles** (p. 53), an alternative: guidelines that frame decisions through a project, from a broad mindset to project-specific requirements. Not built here.
- **Vision cone** (p. 141), a helper: past, now and future, then working back to the next steps. Not built here.
- **Stakeholder map** (p. 83), a helper: who has an interest in the problem and the solution, and how they relate. Not built here.
- **Trend analysis** (p. 119), a helper: developments outside, drawn as lines with sourced signs. Anna parked it for when she researches solutions (Anna, 20.09.2026). Not built here.
- **Measure and evaluate**, a helper: named only, with no page of its own here. Not built here.

## Parked: money, market and approval (p. 138)

The book's template also asks about financial success (sales, revenue, market share, what lenders or partners need), the business case for the major stakeholders, how much success matters to management, and bringing in early the people who sign the project off. Alone, there is no market and no stakeholder besides Anna. So these questions are parked. If what she builds is ever published or sold, they come back as the book has them.
