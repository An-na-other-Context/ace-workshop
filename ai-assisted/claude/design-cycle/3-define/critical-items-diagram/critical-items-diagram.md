## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 145–147):

- **Goal:** give structure to what the first two phases found, and get ready to ideate and experiment.
- **What it is:** eight critical elements, drawn as a daisy. Four are experiences the person must have. Four are functions that must work for them. One of the eight looks at a completely new or future expectation. The book labels the petals "love", "magic" and "happiness" (p. 146, quoted).
- **Around the daisy:** what needfinding (finding out what people need) and benchmarking (looking at how others solve it) brought in. In the middle sits the question of what is critical.
- **It leads to:** the How might we questions that open Ideate.
- **Use it to:** weigh what Understand and Observe found and filter out the critical parts · prepare Ideate and Prototype · settle what is essential · derive several How might we questions.
- **Why the book's expert uses it** (Christian Hohmann, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences and Arts): teams find it hard to pin down what really matters. The diagram is a very simple way to see what is important right now, and it keeps everyone on one line.

## The cycle, alone

1. **Ask what is critical.** Start from what Understand and Observe found: the [problem statement](../../1-understand/problem-statement/problem-statement.md), the [jobs](../../1-understand/jobs-to-be-done/jobs-to-be-done.md), the [AEIOU](../../2-observe/aeiou/aeiou.md) pattern (activities, environments, interactions, objects, users), the low points of the [customer journey map](../../2-observe/customer-journey-map/customer-journey-map.md).
2. **Write candidates.** Experiences on one side, functions on the other. Each one gets a quote or a picture. More than eight is fine here.
3. **Narrow to eight.** Walk the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) out loud over the candidates, until four experiences and four functions remain. One of the eight looks ahead. It counts among the eight; there is no ninth (Anna, 20.09.2026).
4. **Questions, then the clarity check.** Write the How might we questions the eight raise. Then check. All five pass → done. Not yet → back to 2: add elements and try them small, instead of arguing with yourself.

No fixed time. If it drags, keep more elements and test them with small experiments. That is the book's expert advice (p. 147), and it works alone too.

**Keep it in sight.** The book says to display the current diagram (p. 147). Anna's own notes give the reason for one person: what is out of sight drops out of working memory. Pin it where you work, for example on the [design board](../../../../../design/README.md).

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 147–148)

- **It takes rounds.** The eight can't be described perfectly the first time. After each experiment, check whether they still hold. Alone: short cycles, and Reflect looks at them again.
- **Don't get lost in discussion.** Without agreement, add elements and test their importance small. Alone, the discussion is with yourself. Same answer: more elements, small tests.
- **Shorter is better**, ideally no more than 60 minutes. Alone: a guard against perfectionism. Nobody is holding a clock.
- **Display the valid diagram.** Alone: pin it where you work.
- **Pictures and quotes** from interviews or testing enrich the elements. Alone: one quote per element, from your run log.
- **The use case** (p. 148): Lilly's team gathers the crucial factors in the diagram. First they use it to rank the hypotheses (guesses to test) from interviews and analyses. Then they test those in a critical experience prototype and a critical function prototype. Later the diagram sums up what the iterations found. Alone: the hypotheses come from your own tools (jobs, AEIOU pattern, journey low points). Later, in Prototype, one experience and one function get tried small. In Reflect, the diagram comes back.
- **Key learnings** (p. 148): the diagram describes the key elements of a possible solution · accept that they change when new insights or needs turn up. Alone: date the sheet, and expect it to change.

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **Context mapping** (p. 133), an alternative: the surroundings of a problem in categories such as trends, economy, place and technology. Not built here.
- **Vision cone** (p. 141), an alternative: past, now and future, then the way back to a step for today. Not built here.
- **How might we questions** (p. 125), what it feeds: [How might we](../../3-define/how-might-we/how-might-we.md).
- **Empathy map** (p. 93), a helper: what a person sees, hears, thinks and feels, says and does, plus pains and gains. Not built here.
- **Persona / user profile** (p. 97), a helper. Its default boxes for gender and age are on Anna's no list; alone, the user is you. Not built here.
- **Ask 5x why** (ask why five times; p. 67–69), a helper: ask why until the real cause shows. The book keeps asking past the point of discomfort, which is on Anna's no list. The [gentle why](../../supports/gentle-why/gentle-why.md) stops at the first sign of it.
