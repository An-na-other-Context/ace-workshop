## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 177, 179):

- **Goal:** catch the core of an idea quickly and share it with a clear aim.
- **What it is:** NABC (need, approach, benefit, competition) puts four questions around one idea: the need (the problem), the approach (the solution), the benefit, and the competition (what people use today instead). The book calls it the smallest form of a method for structuring a business idea.
- **Where it's used:** in Ideate, when checking prototypes, and to understand users better; also to document ideas and present them.
- **Use it to:** catch an idea's core fast · keep the person in focus, since it starts with their problem · look at an idea from four sides · get early feedback · compare ideas with each other.
- **Why the book's expert picks it** (p. 179): it starts with the person and their problem, and it makes an idea's core clear in a short presentation. Early on it raises the important questions; later it trims a mass of information back down.

## The cycle, alone

One cross per idea that survived the [2x2 matrix](../2x2-matrix/2x2-matrix.md). Need first, always.

1. **Need first.** Who has it (you, in which state), in what situation, with what problem, and the need that follows.
2. **Approach.** How it gets solved, with a sketch, and what it costs you to build and to keep.
3. **Benefit.** For you, and how you'd notice it. The [Define success](../../3-define/define-success/define-success.md) sheet gives the measure.
4. **Instead of today.** What you do today, the other options, and what makes this one different.
5. **A bolder cross.** Once the first cross stands, write a bolder answer to the same need, then compare the two. If both hold, walk the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) to pick which one gets built first.
6. **Clarity check.** All five pass → this cross is what goes into Prototype. Not yet → back to the box an open check points at, starting from the need.

No fixed time (Anna, 19.09.2026). A cross usually fits on one page (p. 180). Stopping is allowed at any step.

### If both crosses hold: walk the matrix (out loud)

The [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md), reworded for competing ideas. It only comes in when two crosses both hold; the cross itself doesn't choose.

1. A new idea, or one that keeps coming back? → goes forward if: keeps coming back
2. Would building it add value right now? → goes forward if: yes
3. Can I build it with what I have? → goes forward if: doable
4. Does it get costlier if I wait? → goes forward if: costlier

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 179–180)

- **Support every description with sketches and drawings.** Alone: a sketch in the approach box.
- **Storytelling often helps carry an NABC.** Storytelling isn't built here; its parts and this cross fit together.
- **In a presentation, the parts can come one after another** instead of as a cross. Alone, the cross is a thinking tool; showing it can wait.
- **Prototypes can be presented quickly this way too.** Reuse the cross in Prototype and Test.
- **Once the problem is described and a solution is known, look on purpose for different, newer ones**, especially for a bigger change. Alone: after the first cross, ask what a bolder answer to the same need would be (step 5).
- **Use case:** ideas get documented and drawn in an NABC, then presented for feedback; the team is reminded to look beyond the obvious competitors. Alone: feedback comes later, in Test, and the alternatives are your own habits, meaning what you do today.
- **Key learnings:** it normally fits on one page (check 4) · describe need, approach, benefit and competition (check 2) · it also prepares an elevator pitch or a value proposition (parked below).

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **Idea communication sheet** (Playbook p. 105), an alternative: title, description, sketch, the simplest form. Not opened for the toolkit, since it is in the other book. Not built here.
- **Lean canvas / business model canvas** (p. 251), an alternative: fuller business structuring, for a market. Not opened; not built here.
- **Storytelling** (p. 129), an alternative and a helper: users, quotes, meaning, conclusion. Not built here.
- **2x2 matrix** (p. 155), what comes before: the ideas worth a cross are the ones that survived it. [2x2 matrix](../2x2-matrix/2x2-matrix.md).
- **Watering hole, green and red feedback** (no page), a helper: green asks which strengths should stay, red asks which weaknesses should improve. Named only; not built here.

## Parked: the pitch and the business half (p. 177–179)

The elevator pitch (p. 179) is the shortest way to present an idea, in three parts: a hook that provokes and wakes interest, the NABC in the middle, and a close that names what comes next and invites the next step. The business half covers how the idea earns money, the business benefit, and the unique selling proposition against competitors. Both exist to convince other people, so they wait until this gets shown outside. The hook and the close are worth reading then.
