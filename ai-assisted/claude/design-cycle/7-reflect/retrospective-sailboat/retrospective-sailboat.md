## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 243, 245):

- **Goal:** look back at how it went, and learn something that makes the next iteration better. At the end of each iteration, and at the end of a project.
- **The picture:** a boat sailing towards an island.
  - **Wind:** what pushed you along, what went well.
  - **Anchor:** what slowed you down.
  - **Cliffs:** risks and hazards you have no power over.
  - **Island:** the shared goal and motivation you're heading for.
- **What it does:** it covers three kinds of force: what speeds you up, what holds you back, and what the surroundings do. It builds a "failure mindset" (p. 243, quoted): mistakes are a chance to change and learn.
- **Where it comes from:** it's common in Scrum (a team way of working in short rounds called sprints) for looking back on a sprint. It works after any design thinking phase or a finished project. It can also be drawn as a board of four quadrants (p. 245): keep doing the same way · do differently next time · try out · not relevant.
- **Why the expert picks it** (Achim Schmidt): most projects stand or fall with motivation, mood and communication. The sailboat helps with them in a kind, structured way, and needs almost no preparation.

## The cycle, alone

Short, after a run. The book's own key learning: hold a short retro (retrospective, a look back) in each iteration too, and not only at the end (p. 246).

1. **Wind first.** What pushed you along. It comes before the anchor.
2. **Anchor, cliffs, island.** What slowed you · what you have no power over · where you're heading.
3. **One cause.** Cluster the anchors and walk the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md) over them, out loud. Then ask one why about what goes forward: enough to reach the cause, never enough to hurt. This is Anna's [gentle why](../../supports/gentle-why/gentle-why.md).
4. **One measure.** Exactly what changes, or gets tried, next time. Then the clarity check. All five pass → close. Not yet → back to 3: cluster, find one cause, then one measure.

Cliffs never go through the matrix. Naming them is the whole point, and nothing is expected of you afterwards. You can stop after any step; the bookmark holds your place.

The book's five steps (p. 244–245): open · collect · cluster and prioritise · define measures · close. Here, the opening is the bookmark and the run being looked at; collecting is 1 and 2; clustering is 3; the measure is 4; the close is the last part of the sheet.

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 245–246)

- **Set a good mood:** start with positive feedback and insights, no blame, everyone heard. Alone: fill the wind field before the anchor field.
- **Have a big retro led by someone outside the team,** at a milestone or in a hard spot. Alone: that's when you can ask Claude to walk it with you, out loud, the way the discernment matrix gets walked.
- **Change the place.** Leave the everyday workplace; a café or a park changes the retro. Alone: somewhere other than the desk where the run went wrong.
- **Build trust.** With sensitive topics, anonymity can help. Ask what unspoken things hold the team back. Alone: what am I not writing down?
- **Don't skip it.** Teams who know each other well tend to leave it out, and lose the good mood it keeps. Alone: working alone is the far end of knowing each other well, so this is the tool most likely to get skipped.
- **Small changes often set off big things.** You can even start with why you hold a retro at all. Alone: one measure per retro is enough.
- **The use case** (p. 246): after each iteration, Lilly's teams sit down briefly and look at what went well and what didn't. She tries new techniques each iteration, knowing not all will work; when one doesn't, the team has learned to laugh about it. Alone: after each run. Trying a tool and parking it again is part of the same habit.
- **Key learnings** (p. 246): a short retro in each iteration too · everyone writes down at least one thing to try · keep the mood positive and trusting, since it's about getting better step by step. Alone: short and often beats long and rare · one thing to try, every time · getting better, with nobody judged.

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **[Feedback capture grid](../../6-test/feedback-capture-grid/feedback-capture-grid.md)** (p. 217), an alternative and a helper: four fields per run. The book closes a retro with one. Kept by Anna (20.09.2026).
- **I like, I wish, I wonder** (p. 239), an alternative: three sentence starters, plus one that asks what if. Not built here.
- **[Lessons learned](../../7-reflect/lessons-learned/lessons-learned.md)** (p. 255), a helper: the written record of what a project taught, the method included.
- **Ask 5x why** (p. 67–69), a helper for step 3: getting to causes. The book keeps asking "until it gets uncomfortable" (p. 69, quoted). That is on Anna's no list, the things she has ruled out; her [gentle why](../../supports/gentle-why/gentle-why.md) stops at the first sign of discomfort (Anna, 18.09.2026).
- **Sketch notes** (p. 173–174), a helper: drawing instead of writing. The [sketch](../../supports/sketch/sketch.md) support card holds its building blocks.
- **Water cooler chats** (no page), an alternative: the book's name for informal talks with colleagues. Named only. Not built here.

## Parked: the group parts (p. 244–245)

The book opens by telling the group what the retro is for. Everyone reads their notes aloud, what's said stays in the room, anonymity helps with sensitive topics, and the group parts with good feelings. A big retro gets someone from outside to lead it. Alone, there's no group to open for or to hear. These parts wait for a run that had more than one person in it. Water cooler chats wait with them.
