## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 255, 256, 257):

- **Goal:** reflect on what was learned and record it, during a project and at its end.
- **What it is:** a written, orderly collection of good and bad experiences, developments, findings, mistakes and risks. It looks at them on several levels: technical and content, emotional and social, and process.
- **Two levels.** *Project level:* what went well and what didn't · which methods were used, how and why · what should change · the key learnings, good and bad. *Meta level* (about the method itself): what design thinking means to you, with its pros, cons and challenges · how it differs from other approaches · how it combines with them · where it could be used in your own surroundings.
- **Use it to:** collect experience in an orderly way · learn from it for the next project · build a positive attitude to mistakes and value progress · keep findings usable instead of losing them.
- **Why the expert picks it** (Stefanie Gerken, HPI (Hasso Plattner Institute) School of Design Thinking): reflecting regularly and turning the results into actions makes projects work. Problems show up early, and what works can be made stronger.

## The cycle, alone

Once per stretch of runs. The [retrospective sailboat](../../7-reflect/retrospective-sailboat/retrospective-sailboat.md) looks at a single run; this tool looks at the stretch. If the last run ended badly, the book says to wait about six weeks (p. 257): from a distance, things can be looked at without stirring everything up. That's the kinder order, and waiting is always allowed.

1. **Project level.** What worked, what didn't, what changes, on three lines: content, feeling, process.
2. **Meta level.** What the method is worth to you, and what it costs.
3. **A few actions.** Less is more. Name what worked as much as what failed.
4. **Put them somewhere,** so they don't close with the file: [Define success](../../3-define/define-success/define-success.md), or the next run's plan. Then the clarity check. All five pass → done. Not yet → back to the step that's missing.

The toolkit also writes the actions into its ledger. Where that part goes in ACE Workshop is *open, not decided for ACE Workshop yet.*

The book's two steps (p. 256) are 1 and 2. Step 3 comes from the expert's tips (p. 257); step 4 is Claude's suggestion.

No matrix walk here: the limit does the choosing. A few actions, not a list; the rest waits in the parking lot. You can stop after any step; the bookmark holds your place.

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 257–258)

- **Prepare key questions:** what exactly is being looked at, and when? Where's the focus? Who leads, and who's invited? Alone: what am I looking at, and which runs?
- **If the mood is bad, wait about six weeks.** From a distance, many things can be looked at calmly. Alone: the most useful line here. A bad run doesn't get reflected on the same night.
- **Plan lessons learned into the project,** and collect them at milestones too. Alone: once per stretch of runs.
- **Less is more.** Stress what worked and what failed, and set actions from both. Don't dwell only on the negative, and don't get personal. Alone: that holds for yourself as much as for anyone.
- **It's a good base for design principles or for Define success.** Alone: Define success is one of Anna's kept tools (20.09.2026), so the actions can feed it.
- **The use case** (p. 258): Lilly's teams reflect at regular intervals and at key milestones. The meta-level lessons get gathered across teams, so design principles can be adjusted and teamwork improved. Alone: the meta level gathers across your own stretches of runs instead. Where it gets gathered: *open, not decided for ACE Workshop yet.* (In the toolkit, the ledger holds it.)
- **Key learnings** (p. 258): use it on several levels: technical and content, emotional and social, process · hold reflection sessions during the project with a log book, and add the meta level at the end · prepare key questions beforehand. Alone: content, feeling and process, on three lines · the run logs are the log book · the sheet prepares the questions for you.

- **Lean canvas** (p. 251) and **Create a pitch** (p. 247), helpers on the market and audience side. Not built here.

## Parked: the workshop (p. 256–258)

The book holds lessons learned as a workshop. The core team and other stakeholders review the project together, someone leads, guests get invited, and meta-level lessons get gathered across teams. Alone, the project level has one voice, and the run logs stand in for the other accounts. These parts wait for the day there's a team. The market and audience tools the book names, the lean canvas (p. 251) and creating a pitch (p. 247), wait with them.
