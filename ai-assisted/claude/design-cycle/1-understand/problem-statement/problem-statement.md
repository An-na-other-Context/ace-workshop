> **AI-made.** Drafted by Claude (AI) from Anna (@annaothercrow)'s solo design-thinking toolkit, which adapts *The Design Thinking Toolbox* for one person. The choices are Anna's. See [Who made what](#who-made-what) and [Sources](#sources).

Board version: [problem-statement.excalidraw](problem-statement.excalidraw). Open it in Excalidraw or in VS Code.

## What it's for

The book's description, in other words (Toolbox p. 49, 51):

- **Goal:** define the key problem clearly, in one simple sentence.
- **Rule:** start with the problem, never with a solution.
- **Place:** it marks where the problem space starts and ends, and hands over to Ideate.
- **Use it to:** share one understanding of a problem · turn findings into a design challenge · give ideas a direction and a frame · build "How might we …" questions on it · keep a yardstick to measure success against later.
- **When problems show up** (p. 28): mostly in Understand and Observe, but new ones turn up in every phase.

## The cycle, alone

1. **Write a few** definitions. Each one answers: what the problem is, why it's a problem, who has the need, when and where, how it's solved today.
2. **Walk the matrix** over each definition, out loud: the [discernment matrix](../../supports/discernment-matrix/discernment-matrix.md). What passes goes forward; the rest goes to the parking lot.
3. **Merge** into one sentence: *How might we [what] for [whom] so that [their need] is met?*
4. **Clarity check.** All four pass → done. Not yet → back to 1 and write a few more.

No fixed time and no fixed number of definitions: short cycles until the sentence passes the checks (Anna, 19.09.2026).

## The four clarity checks, and where they come from

| Check | What the book says |
|---|---|
| 1 · Problem ≠ cause ≠ effect | Problems, causes and effects often get muddled together; create structural clarity. Problem and solution also shape each other as a project goes on. (p. 51) |
| 2 · Person + need, no solution | A good statement centres people and their needs. Functions, sales or a specific technology may add to it but shouldn't lead; naming a technology early narrows the ideas. (p. 28, 49) |
| 3 · Right size | Broad enough to leave room for creative freedom, narrow enough to solve with the resources you have. (p. 28) |
| 4 · Clear to me tomorrow | Everybody on the team must understand it (p. 28). Alone, the "team" is you on another day. (Claude's wording) |

## Expert tips and use case, alone (p. 51–52)

- **Reframe the problem.** Alone you're also the sponsor, so each cycle may reframe freely.
- **Write statements from different perspectives.** Your own: a calm day and an overloaded day, the start and the end of a session.
- **Keep problem, cause and effect apart.** That's clarity check 1; the sheet gives each its own line.
- **Hold an early picture of a good result.** That's the side note under the merge, kept out of the sentence so check 2 stays true.
- **The use case asks W+H questions** (who, what, when, where, why, how). The definition questions in step 1 are W+H questions.
- **Key learnings:** a clear definition often takes several rounds · enjoy precise wording · create variants. Here: the cycles, the clarity checks, and variants from your own perspectives.

## Tools the book names alongside it

- **Design brief** (p. 29), an alternative: the full task description; the problem statement is its minimum.
- **Design principles** (p. 53), an alternative: guidelines that frame decisions across a project.
- **Context mapping** (p. 133), a helper: how an everyday experience is perceived, when, with whom, in what context.
- **Cause-effect diagrams** (p. 49), a helper: named only, no page of their own.
- **5W+H questions** (p. 71), a helper: who, what, when, where, why, how. [5W+H](../../supports/5w-h/5w-h.md).
- **How might we questions** (p. 125), a helper: turns a found need into a design challenge. [How might we](../../3-define/how-might-we/how-might-we.md).

## Parked: dot voting (p. 159–162)

In a team of 5–10, everyone places 3–5 dots on the options that best meet criteria agreed beforehand; the options with the most dots get regrouped and decided openly. Alone there's nobody to vote with, so the discernment matrix does the choosing. Three of dot voting's parts already live here: criteria first (the matrix questions), writing kept apart from judging (steps 1 and 2), and the parking lot. The book's other choosing tool is the [2x2 matrix](../../4-ideate/2x2-matrix/2x2-matrix.md) (p. 155–156).
