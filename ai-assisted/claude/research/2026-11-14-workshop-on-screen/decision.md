## The question (annaothercrow)

How can a real workshop be emulated on screen, since a real one is out of reach for now? How do real workshops teach, what carries over to a screen, and what has to change so it is accessible?

## What the research shows (AI summary of research.html)

- For understanding concepts, a screen does about as well as hands; in one large meta-analysis adults even learned better on screen (F10). Hands still win for handling tools, motor skills and messy real faults (F9, F11).
- Guidance matters more than the medium (F9). Guidance built into the scene, "guided without feeling guided", is one middle path (F13).
- Hardware and repair games are liked but unproven; they teach how parts fit and come apart, not how to find a fault (F20–F23).
- Access: getting to a workshop costs energy and money (F24, F33); predictability, several ways in, little text, adjustable speed and undo help (F25–F32).
- Adults learning alone, and people with motor or learning disabilities, are hardly studied.

## What it could mean for ACE Workshop (AI interpretation, to check)

- A workshop on screen is a reasonable base for understanding how machines work; real handling skills stay outside it.
- Guidance can come from the scene itself rather than from written instructions.
- Fault-finding needs its own design, since games rarely teach it.
