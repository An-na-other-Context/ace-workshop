Real workshop on screen

How real workshops teach, what carries over to a screen, and what has to change so it is accessible. Findings are numbered (F1…), so they can be picked by number.

In short

Real workshops teach through people and objects.An expert who shows their thinking, responsibility handed over bit by bit, a real object you care about, instant physical feedback, cheap failure.
For understanding, a screen does about as well as hands.Adults even learned better on screen in one large meta-analysis. Hands still win for handling tools, motor skills and messy real faults. Both combined beat either alone.
Guidance matters more than the medium.Free exploration overloads beginners; guidance built into the scene ("guided without feeling guided") is one middle path.
Hardware games are loved but unproven.They assume prior knowledge and explain little; repair games teach how parts fit and come apart, not how to find a fault.
Adults learning alone are hardly studied,and neither are motor or learning disabilities. First-person accounts fill some gaps: reassembly order, energy cost of getting to a workshop, money, amount of text.
Q1 · Real workshopsHow real workshops teach. F1–F8
  1. F1Apprenticeship, made visible: modelling, coaching, scaffolding, fading. "Global before local": tailors' apprentices first assemble pre-cut pieces, and learn to cut later. Cognitive apprenticeship adds saying your reasoning out loud, reflecting, then exploring alone. Review
  2. F2Start at the edge of real work: newcomers begin with small, low-risk tasks that still count, and move toward the centre (Lave & Wenger 1991). Study summary only
  3. F3Craft knowledge lives in the body: learned by watching, copying and repeating; much can't be put into words (Marchand). Study summary only
  4. F4Repair cafés (Copenhagen): "educational repair" breaks tasks into parts and invites the visitor in; "diagnostic repair" with a multimeter and system logic feels like a puzzle. The object is the visitor's own, often with a story. Study
  5. F5Repair café (UK): repairers switch between intuition and systematic checking; their know-how is rarely captured anywhere. Study
  6. F6Inclusive maker club: with checklists, visual templates and self-chosen projects, nearly all 18 autistic students finished and presented; but their interest grew less than peers', some got very frustrated, and no single support worked for everyone. Study
  7. F7Taking apart a discarded PC: a withdrawn teen sorted and documented each part, charted the reassembly, found a burnt-out video card and got it running. Example
  8. F8Autistic repairers: troubleshooting feels logical and predictable; a failed repair costs little. Lived "Put it back the way it came apart" never worked for an autistic ADHD writer (sequencing); one hint ("locks in place from the top down") did more than the manual. Lived
Q2 · On screenWhat carries over, what's lost, what's gained. F9–F23

Reviews and studies

  1. F9de Jong, Linn & Zacharia 2013: no difference for concepts in many studies; hands give practical skills and troubleshooting; screens can highlight, simplify, change time and show the invisible. Guidance is "the most crucial variable"; combining both is "likely to be optimal". Review
  2. F1035 studies, 3,303 learners: no overall difference; adults learned better on screen; when touch carried no information, screen was better. Two thirds of the studies were physics. Review
  3. F11Physics, 58 studies: combined real + virtual beat either alone; virtual did better on abstract concepts, real on handling instruments. Collaboration was designed in only 15%. Review
  4. F12PhET circuit kit: students who used the simulation built a real circuit faster later. Study · Interviews: learning happens when the learner's own questions drive the play; anything without a learning purpose distracts. Study
  5. F13Implicit scaffolding: guiding through what the scene allows, limits, hints and answers back, instead of written instructions. "Guided without feeling guided." Study summary onlyMine may suit PDA: guidance without demands.
  6. F14Engineering, 22 publications: virtual labs help, but "lack the ability to completely replace hands-on labs"; some reported effects are implausibly large. Review abstract only

Representations and pacing (close to dive and climb)

  1. F15Parts alone first, then together worked better for some learners (Pollock, Chandler & Sweller 2002). Study summary only
  2. F16Arrows mean "what happens": diagrams without arrows are read as structure, with arrows as function (Heiser & Tversky 2006). Study summary only
  3. F17Animation rarely beats a still image showing the same; it is often too fast. Letting the learner control it may help. Review summary only
  4. F18Learner-paced segments help, especially for complex, fast, new material. Review summary only
  5. F19Trying before being taught ("productive failure") beat teaching first across 53 studies; younger learners did better taught first. Review summary only

Games and courses

  1. F20nand2tetris (build a computer from logic gates up): taught at 400+ places, "demanding but incredibly rewarding" (quote from learner reviews summary only); no formal study of what learners learn. Example
  2. F21Turing Complete, NandGame: praised, but they expect Boolean algebra and explain little; newcomers get stuck early. Lived Opinion
  3. F22Car repair games teach how parts fit, the order they come apart, sizes and torque; they "can't teach you how to figure out what's wrong". Opinion PC Building Simulator: one weak class comparison. Study
  4. F23Assemble with Care, a calm repair game: praised for mood, criticised as slow and shallow. Example summary only

Lost on screen

  • touch, where it carries information
  • handling tools, motor skill
  • messy real faults and diagnosis
  • natural pauses that push planning
  • a mentor in the room

Gained on screen

  • time that slows or stops; the invisible made visible
  • clutter removed, what matters highlighted
  • many tries, no fear of breaking
  • own pace, near-zero cost
  • actions that can shape the help given
Q3 · AccessWhat has to change so it works for the learner profile. F24–F33
  1. F24Barriers for disabled makers: outreach, physical access, money, information, belonging. Getting there can leave "no strength" for the work; tools are built for a tall standing man; all disabled makers called access to information critical. Study
  2. F25Makerspaces for autistic youth: predictability, several ways in, equal belonging, quiet zones, visual tool labels. No autistic youth were interviewed. Study summary only
  3. F26Supports that helped weakest executive function most: vocabulary cards, "I can" checkpoints, a "flashlight" that highlights what matters, visual organisers, a clean consistent interface (children). Study
  4. F27Dyslexic software engineers: "I fear documentation the most"; diagrams, flowcharts, text-to-speech and reading a little then trying it right away helped. Study
  5. F28Virtual lab with students with disabilities (7): a sense of safety, own pace, visuals in several forms; less anxiety. Study abstract only · Across 36 studies, learning and motor disabilities are almost absent. Review
  6. F29Hyperfocus in ADHD adults: started by interest, hard to start or stop at will, long stretches need recovery. Study · Monotropism: attention pulled strongly to few interests at a time, a theory from autistic people. Lived
  7. F30Game Accessibility Guidelines: adjustable speed, simple remappable controls, no holding buttons, practice without failure, recall the current goal, replay instructions, hide background motion, no sudden surprises. Guideline
  8. F31W3C cognitive accessibility: undo, few interruptions, no reliance on memory, user controls when things move, simpler personal views. Guideline
  9. F32Dyslexia style: sans-serif, 1.5 line spacing, extra letter spacing, plain pastel background. Guideline summary only · VR risks for autistic users: sensory overload, dizziness; adjustable stimulus. Review summary only
  10. F33Cost: screen labs are cheap, nand2tetris and NandGame free; money is a core barrier to physical making. Study

GlossarySpecial words on this page.
Meta-analysis
A study that pools the results of many studies to see the overall effect.
Effect size (g)
How big a difference is: around 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
Scaffolding
Temporary help that is taken away as the learner gets better (fading).
Cognitive apprenticeship
Learning like an apprentice, with the expert's thinking made visible, not only their hands.
Tacit knowledge
Know-how that is hard to put into words, like how a part feels when it clicks in.
PhET
Free science simulations from the University of Colorado.
Productive failure
Letting learners try a problem before teaching them the method.
Hyperfocus
Very deep, long focus on one interest, hard to steer at will.
Monotropism
A theory, developed by autistic people, that attention gathers strongly on few things at a time.
Abstract
The short summary at the top of a research paper.
Search snippet
The few lines a search engine shows under each result.
Second-hand
A page that reports someone else's study instead of being the study itself.
Saved copy
When the web reader showed a PDF as raw code, the helper opened the PDF file the reader had saved on this PC.
Repository
A university's online archive of papers.
Preprint, arXiv
A paper shared before other researchers have checked it; arXiv is a free online archive for them.
PubMed Central
A free archive of medical and life-science papers, run by the US National Library of Medicine.
403, 404
Web error codes: 403 = the site refused the request, 404 = the page wasn't found.
Redirect
A web address that sends you on to a different address.
