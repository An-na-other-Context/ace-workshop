Dive down, climb back up

In short

  • No published method covers the whole loop. The closest is "semantic waves" from computing teaching: down from the abstract word to concrete examples and back up. It even names the profile's risk: going down and never repacking ("down escalator").
  • Bottom-up computing courses exist (nand2tetris, Petzold's Code, Ben Eater's breadboard computer). nand2tetris starts at the top, traces a running game down to logic gates, then builds back up. No outcome studies for any of them.
  • Asking why and explaining to yourself help (medium effects), but asking why is weak where you know little: exactly a brand-new bottom layer.
  • The clearest warning is unguided depth. Exploring with guidance and a summing-up step wins; without help it loses. The help needed changes per layer.
  • Research on people like the profile mostly fits the model (monotropism, autistic flow, dyslexic "exploring"), and names the risks: transitions, getting stuck, interruptions, a slower whole picture. Much of it is theory, interviews and first-person accounts.

Dive

Backed by: semantic waves (unpacking), diving as a natural coping move, asking why and self-explanation, curiosity aiding memory, monotropism, Temple Grandin's bottom-up thinking. Watch for: "why" is weak at a new layer; interesting but off-goal detail lowers learning; unguided discovery does worse; hard to leave once deep.

Check

Backed by: metacognition (+7 months of progress in school studies), "explain it" exposes false understanding, nand2tetris tests at every layer, check-ins tailored to the person. Watch for: we all overrate our understanding; alarms and lists often fail with autistic inertia; a check from outside can break flow, so it works better from inside the task.

Climb

Backed by: semantic waves (repacking), concreteness fading, worked examples with "name the principle" prompts, 4C/ID (large effect), learning by teaching, nand2tetris building up. Watch for: never repacking; putting the whole together may simply take longer for autistic thinkers; help has to shrink as you master a layer.
ApproachesMethods that match parts of the loop. A1–A12
  1. A1Semantic waves: unpack an abstract term into concrete examples and simple words, then repack it into the term. Failure modes: "high flatline" (only abstract: the profile's "top-down"), "low flatline" (only concrete, never linked up), "down escalator" (down, then moving on). Lesson analyses, no outcome trials. Theory Example summary only
  2. A2"Up and down the ladder of abstraction" (Bret Victor): step down to single cases to see why, step up to see the whole range, repeat. An essay for designers. Opinion
  3. A3nand2tetris: week 1 traces a running game down to NAND gates, then builds up through logic, ALU, memory, machine code, compiler, operating system. Every module has a test script: a built-in check per layer. Its bottom is the logic gate, not electronics. Example
  4. A4Petzold's Code (flashlights and relays up to a CPU) and Ben Eater's 8-bit breadboard computer: bottom-up builds, no outcome studies. Example summary only · Julia Evans: everything on a computer happens for a logical reason; learning can start from a question or a bug. Lived
  5. A5Spiral curriculum (Bruner): topics return, deeper each time. Fits the profile's repeated passes, but usually starts shallow; criticised for staying superficial and for simplifications that turn into misconceptions. Theory
  6. A6Concreteness fading: from acting it out, to a picture, to the symbol. Positive in maths and science; may not carry over to every subject. Review
  7. A7Productive failure: struggle first, then teaching (53 studies, g 0.36, higher with a faithful design). The teaching afterwards is part of the method; reversed for young children. Review summary only
  8. A8Cognitive apprenticeship: modelling, coaching, help that's withdrawn, saying it out loud, reflecting, exploring. No verified meta-analysis. Theory summary only
  9. A9Learning by building (Papert): fits "can I build it". Weak evidence; across 164 studies, discovery without help did worse than teaching, discovery with guidance did better. Opinion Review summary only
  10. A104C/ID: whole realistic tasks from simple to complex, with background knowledge (the why and how), just-in-time steps, practice of routines, and help that shrinks. Large effect (d 0.79), strongest in higher education. Review summary only
  11. A11Asking why, explaining to yourself, teaching: self-explanation g 0.55; asking why d 0.56 and better with prior knowledge; at low knowledge, picturing it did better. Preparing to teach g 0.35, teaching g 0.56. Review
  12. A12Illusion of explanatory depth: people overrate how well they understand a device until they try to explain it. Supports "explain it" as a check. Study summary only · Diving is a natural move: learners who can't handle a concept drop to a more concrete level on their own (Hazzan). Study summary only
People like the profileAutistic, ADHD and dyslexic learners in research and in their own words. P1–P12

Monotropism and flow

  1. P1Monotropism: attention gathers in a few deep tunnels; learning follows interest; switching is hard; parts aren't enough without how they fit. Helps: building on interests, less time pressure, time to switch. Theory
  2. P2Measuring it: people who are both autistic and ADHD scored highest on a monotropism questionnaire (preprint); lab results since are mixed. Study
  3. P3Autistic flow (10 adults): it helps self-regulation; unpredictable interruptions are distressing and spoil later flow; predictable times and places helped. Study
  4. P4Autistic inertia (32 adults): starting is hardest, stopping and switching hard too; willpower doesn't fix it. Helped: another person nearby, tiny first steps, routines. Usually not: alarms, lists, reminders. "Never coming back up" may be inertia, not a lack of discipline. Study

In their own words

  1. P5Temple Grandin: "All my thinking is bottom-up instead of top-down": details assembled into concepts; needs lots of data; struggles with what she can't picture. Lived
  2. P6A monotropic rabbit hole (mentions ADHD, PDA, monotropism): thrilling, torn between tangents and covering ground, uneasy about unfinished parts of the mental map; plans pushed aside. What helped: turning the dive into open questions, setting intentions. Lived
  3. P7Map of monotropic experiences: names "rabbit holes of research" and inertia that leaves you unable to start or to stop; transitions need time. Lived

Detail first, ADHD, dyslexia

  1. P8Detail-first, disputed: older reviews found a strong pull to local detail; a 2015 meta-analysis found no detail advantage, but the whole picture forms more slowly. So the climb may simply need more time. Review summary only
  2. P9Hyperfocus in ADHD: set off by engaging tasks, time disappears, hard to leave; only about 7 studies. Review · People with more ADHD traits explored more in a foraging game and still earned more. Study summary only
  3. P10Working memory: moderate limits in ADHD adults, so warnings about overload apply strongly. Review summary only · Tailored check-ins help; generic adjustments often don't (25 interview studies). Review summary only
  4. P11Dyslexia as exploring: strong gist and big picture, weaker automatic routines; learning is more conscious and connecting, but slower (partly speculative). Theory
  5. P12Dyslexic students more often learned deeply, but reported picking out main ideas less, which may matter for the "still on track?" check. Study summary only
Needs and limitsWhat the loop needs to work, and where it fails. N1–N7
  1. N1Guidance and a summing-up step: discovery with help wins, without help loses; productive failure only works when teaching follows. Review summary only
  2. N2Help per layer: help that aids beginners hinders those who already know a topic (expertise reversal). The learner is a beginner at each new bottom layer and closer to expert higher up. Review summary only
  3. N3Depth needs a goal: interesting but off-goal detail lowered learning across 58 studies (short sessions); curiosity itself improves memory. Review Study summaries only
  4. N4"Why" needs something to build on: weak where you know little; picturing it or a concrete example may work better there (A11).
  5. N5The climb has to be built in: otherwise it becomes the "down escalator" (A1); fading steps without "name the principle" prompts doesn't reach new kinds of problems. Study summary only
  6. N6Stopping points: layer boundaries with tests (nand2tetris) work like save points; for autistic learners, predictable exits, time to switch, and another person nearby rather than alarms (P4).
  7. N7Black boxes: at some point upper layers have to be used without opening them; learners who keep opening the box can stall. Study summary only
Open questionsPossible experiments, depth 3. 10, none chosen.

Collected by the research helper from what the sources leave open. A menu, nothing decided. No study has tested the whole loop with neurodivergent learners, so the learner's own notes could be the first data.

  1. E1Top first or bottom first: the nand2tetris way (see the whole, then dive) against a pure bottom start.
  2. E2A concrete check: one line linking back to the top question, plus "explain it". Does it catch false understanding?
  3. E3Exits: save points at layer boundaries with a breadcrumb trail back up, against timers; another person nearby.
  4. E4A stopping rule for depth: "can I predict the layer above from this one?"
  5. E5Help per layer in ACE Workshop: more at a new bottom layer, less where mastered.
  6. E6A tangent log: do tangents help or hurt what stays?
  7. E7Climb time: extra time and visual maps for putting the whole together; audio for reading load.
  8. E8"Down escalator" as a design check for every lesson in the game.
  9. E9At a new layer: picturing it or a concrete example, against "why" questions.
  10. E10Write down the loop as it gets used, to compare with these findings later.

GlossarySpecial words on this page.
Semantic waves
Moving between abstract words and concrete examples, down and back up, while teaching.
Repacking
Putting concrete understanding back into the abstract term, the climb.
NAND gate
A tiny logic switch; every part of a computer can be built from many of them.
ALU
Arithmetic logic unit: the part of a processor that calculates.
Metacognition
Thinking about your own thinking: planning, checking and judging how you learn.
Expertise reversal
Help that supports beginners gets in the way of people who already know the topic.
Black box
A part you use by what it does, without opening it to see how.
Effect size (g, d)
How big a difference is: about 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
Monotropism
A theory from autistic people: attention gathers strongly on few things at a time.
Autistic inertia
Difficulty starting, stopping or switching activities, not fixed by willpower.
Meta-analysis
A study that pools the results of many studies to see the overall effect.
Meta-ethnography
A study that pools the findings of many interview studies.
Abstract
The short summary at the top of a research paper.
Search snippet
The few lines a search engine shows under each result.
Second-hand
A page that reports someone else's study instead of being the study itself.
Saved copy
When the web reader showed a PDF as raw code, the helper opened the PDF file the reader had saved on this PC.
Repository
A university's online archive of papers.
Preprint, arXiv
A paper shared before other researchers have checked it; arXiv is a free online archive for them.
PubMed, PubMed Central
Free archives of medical and life-science papers, run by the US National Library of Medicine.
CORE
A free search service for open-access research papers.
403
A web error code: the site refused the request.
Redirect
A web address that sends you on to a different address.
Cookie wall
A page that shows nothing until you accept its cookies.
