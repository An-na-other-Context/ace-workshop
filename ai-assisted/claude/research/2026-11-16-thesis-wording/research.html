In short

No agreed formula for a "design thesis".None of the sources uses "design thesis" as a defined term. They speak of a research question, a problem framing, a preferred state or a claim (F1, F9).
Parts that come up again and again.A short question or problem; its context (why it matters, what exists); the method; the outcome and who it is for (Candy, F5). Plus the preferred state and why it is better (Zimmerman et al., F1).
The question is expected to change.Questions grow out of the making and "drift"; in one case the final question was worded only after the fourth artefact worked (F7, F8, F10). Claims stay provisional (F3, summary only).
When the designer is also the user.First-person research asks for clear boundaries, an interpretive step and a link to wider themes, so accounts become findings (F17, F19).
Repos as research documents exist; wording rules don't.Game researchers keep design reasoning in a repo, a dated public journal or playtest reports (F23–F25). No source gave rules for question grammar (open or closed, sub-questions, "so that"), and nothing on this topic came from ADHD, dyslexic or PDA researchers (F14, F22, F29).
Time boundaries: stated openly, no standard length.First-person studies run from weeks to a decade and mostly end for outside reasons (F33, F35). Planning guides use milestones, a plan that can change and "what if" questions; buffer percentages are rules of thumb (F40–F43). A time box helps when scope can shrink to fit it (F45). Nothing found on planning with dates for ADHD, autistic, dyslexic or PDA adults (F48).
Reframing: every framing found puts the problem outside the person.In the design (mismatch), in institutions as they are now (systemic mismatch), in the abilities a system assumes (ability-based design), in who gets to design (design justice), or in the idea of one "normal" and the power behind it (critical disability studies, neurodiversity paradigm). Critics warn that real effects such as pain can drop out of view (F51–F62).
Q1 · The thesisWhat a thesis-like statement is called and which parts it has. F1–F9
  1. F1Preferred state instead of thesis: an RtD project frames a concrete problem and describes a "preferred state"; the artefact shows what "ought to be". This is the closest thing to a thesis in this paper. Zimmerman et al. 2007
  2. F2Four lenses for judging the work: process (enough detail and reasons for the methods), invention, relevance (why the preferred state is better) and extensibility (others can build on it). Zimmerman et al. 2007
  3. F3Claims stay provisional: RtD theory is "provisional, contingent, aspirational"; theory can live as notes on real design examples (annotated portfolios). Gaver 2012 summary only
  4. F4Practice-based or practice-led: if the made thing is the basis of the contribution, it is practice-based; if the work mainly brings new understanding about practice, practice-led. The two terms are often swapped. Candy 2006
  5. F5Parts of a framing: the problem (a short statement of the question), the context (the work behind it and why it matters), the method, and the outcomes (what they add, and for whom). Citing a UK funding body (AHRB, 2000): define the questions, give context, name methods with reasons. Candy 2006
  6. F6What to avoid: purely private, unspoken knowing isn't research in the doctoral sense; the knowledge has to be shareable and open to challenge. Candy 2006
  7. F7Worded late: in one master's project, the first three artefacts failed; the final question was worded only once the fourth worked. Writing things down made the researcher explain choices. Advice: re-check dropped ideas, notice when to switch and which switch to make. One case, so weak evidence. Reeker et al.
  8. F8Drifting is normal: design research often moves away from its first question on purpose, towards results nobody expected. Krogh names five ways of drifting (accumulative, comparative, serial, expansive, probing). Krogh & Koskinen summary only; second-hand in Reeker et al.
  9. F9Constructive design research works in lab, field or showroom; one chapter is called "Physical Hypotheses and Design" (table of contents only). "Constructive design research" and RtD name similar work. Koskinen et al. 2011 summary only Alrøe & Krogh 2025
Q2 · Research questionsHow the questions relate to the thesis and to the making. F10–F14
  1. F10Questions grow from the making: in practice-based research, questions and design criteria come up while making. Candy & Edmonds 2018 summary only
  2. F11One question, the artefact as evidence: the Delft case ended with a single question of the form "Can a … process be created, such that …?", answered by the working artefact. Reeker et al.
  3. F12How might we is a different tool: design-thinking guides place "How might we" questions after research and synthesis, as a start for ideas. They assume a solution is possible and leave room for many answers. digital.gov Canada School WA CX Greenbook Vanderbilt summary only
  4. F13Games: prototype plans follow the scope of the research questions Eladhari & Ollila 2012 summary only. Game design is often reported only as a tool for other questions; "research through game design" treats the design process itself as where knowledge is made, with no wording rules for questions. Bates et al. 2026
  5. F14Gap: no source gave rules on open or closed questions, main and sub-questions, or "so that" parts in design research questions. Only F5 and F11 touch on form. helper's search result
Q3 · Designer as userFirst-person research: researching your own process. F15–F22
  1. F15What it is: research with data and experience from the researcher themselves: autoethnography, autobiographical design, autoethnographic RtD. Lucero et al. 2019
  2. F16Common, rarely reported: 11 interviews found autobiographical design is common but seldom written up; it rests on long, real use by the people building the system. Neustaedter & Sengers 2012 summary only
  3. F17An interpretive step is needed: without it, detailed accounts stay accounts and add little knowledge (Chien & Hassenzahl, second-hand). Lucero et al. 2019
  4. F18Role tension: balancing roles such as designer, researcher and parent (Desjardins & Ball, second-hand). Lucero et al. 2019
  5. F19Seven criteria: study boundaries (time, place, project type, point of view), authenticity, plausibility, criticality, self-revealing writing, weaving material with personal content, and generalisability. Lucero's own focus and words came from first writing look-back accounts. Lucero et al. 2021 pp. 79–86 read
  6. F20Not a shortcut: it takes dedication, documentation and resilience. Lucero et al. 2021
  7. F21Autistic first-person design research: little is written on how autoethnography is actually done, and autistic practice varies. The authors built recorders so data is caught with little effort and analysed later. Alrøe & Krogh 2025 pp. 1–3 read
  8. F22Gap: none of these gives wording rules for the aim of a first-person study; "how to plan a first-person project" is listed as an open goal. Lucero et al. 2019
Q4 · Working examplesDesign research kept as a living document or repo. F23–F28
  1. F23Design reasoning in the repo: the "Method for Design Materialization" keeps design reasoning in one repository; commit messages link changes to design goals. Khaled & Barr 2023 summary only; also described in Bates et al. 2026
  2. F24Playtest reflections for Godot: "Reflection at Design Actualization" is an open tool for Unity and Godot that records playtests and turns tagged reflections into reports. It starts from a research goal; linking logs to commits is named as future work. Demleitner et al. 2026 first ~70% read
  3. F25A public process journal: dated entries mixing decisions, goals as to-do lists, reflections and links to commits. No research question is written out; the aim stays implicit. Barr 2024 first ~85% read; talk Barr summary only
  4. F26Annotated portfolio of games: four games, with what knowledge came out of designing them; process detail left out on purpose. Bates et al. 2026 pp. 1–5 read
  5. F27A timeline picture: artefacts, experiments and theories mapped over seven months, dropped ones marked. Reeker et al.
  6. F28Reflective documentation: a written record (diary or blog), an overview chart, set review points. Ask what was proposed, decided and carried through, what blocked, and what failures taught. Candy 2006

  1. F29Accounts exist, not on wording: first-person accounts by autistic and ADHD researchers turned up, but none covers wording research questions or scoping self-directed research. One read account: a doctorate is largely self-led and unclear at first; a weekly plan and a visual map helped, and asking directly when instructions were unclear. Simply Psychology. Others seen as snippets only: BPS White Rose essay Autistica (Beth) Autistica (Kana Grace) eLife K. Williams summary only

  2. F33Lengths vary widely: many field studies run weeks; 2 years counts as very long in HCI; an autoethnography can run weeks, months, years or a decade. Lucero et al. 2021 pp. 85, 90
  3. F34The examples in the table come from the same chapter's three case accounts. Lucero et al. 2021 pp. 80–95 · Light Touch: Gaver & Gaver 2023 summary only
  4. F35Endings mostly come from outside: personal circumstances, student training and grant funding, or "temporarily finished" with work going on. The chapter suggests weighing research needs together with personal needs; long first-person studies are hard to fund and to stay committed to. Lucero et al. 2021 pp. 90–91, 97
  5. F36Fuzzy boundaries are named as normal in first-person projects, in time and in scope. One author chose to end, another chose to stay open. Lucero et al. 2021 p. 93
  6. F37Time is often under-reported in RtD: long deployments rarely fit funding or PhD cycles (3–4 years); first-person work can avoid many of these problems. The authors propose 6–12 month deployments. A workshop proposal, not a study. Berger et al. 2025
  7. F38No standard length for an RtD thesis: only semester structures (a three-semester design thesis) and a weekly thesis table turned up. UniBZ NYU ITP summary only
  8. F39First iterations shift expectations: a guide for design-science master's theses reports that expectations changed a lot in the first iteration and that understanding the real problem took long. Constructive master's thesis guide summary only
  9. F40Plan, and let the plan change: plan at the start and during the project; break the work down; a Gantt chart; milestones to check progress; "the project plan can be changed". The example plan runs in quarters over 3.5 years. Warren 2017 slides 1–12, 22–33 of 33
  10. F41Contingency as "what if" questions: what if something arrives late, a tool is missing, the person you rely on is away. No buffer percentage. Warren 2017
  11. F42Four planning levels: the whole project, the coming year, the next months, each day. Expect non-linear progress; plan regular work rather than a last-minute push. Templates for accountability when working alone. No mention of buffers. Federation University
  12. F43Buffer numbers are rules of thumb: 10–15% per phase, contingency weeks after drafts, a 2–4 week block before the final date. From a company blog and a training deck, not research. ONES blog Liverpool slides summary only
  13. F44Gap: nothing specific to design theses or to researchers without a supervisor; the guides assume one. helper's search result
  14. F45Time-boxing: the time is fixed and the scope flexes; each time box has its own deliverable, ideally in 2–4 week rounds. When work doesn't fit, scope gets cut, from a ranked list (MoSCoW). A box that is only a self-set target, with no agreed partial outcomes, is "of little value". A project-management source, not design research. Mosaic Projects
  15. F46Check the date first: build a normal schedule first, to see how realistic a fixed date is. Mosaic Projects
  16. F47Long studies change things too: tiredness with collecting data, lost data; the theory, the made thing and the researcher's life all change over the years. Lucero et al. 2021 pp. 93–97

Q7 · Reframing the issueFramings that place the problem in the design or the system, not in the living being. F51–F75 · added in version 4
Anna (16.11.2026)It's not the baby's, teenager's, adult's, animal's or elderly person's fault that it can't access the thing others seemingly can. It's a design problem. The human rights model applies only where design has failed to accommodate.

Mismatch

  1. F51Mismatch: a gap between a person's needs and the design of a product, place, system or service. It depends on the situation (context, lighting, device, how someone prefers to learn), so a designer can fix it. Asks: design for adaptability and flexibility; note your own mismatches. IDRC
  2. F52Three inclusive design principles: recognise exclusion (it comes from mismatches and the team's own assumptions), learn from diversity, solve for one and extend to many. Microsoft Inclusive Design. Holmes's book reportedly takes "mismatch" from the WHO's 2001 view of disability as an interaction between body and environment Holmes 2018 summary only. The persona spectrum (permanent, temporary, situational) wasn't confirmed: the guidebook link gave 404.
  3. F53Systemic mismatch: a mismatch between a person's traits and what institutions can accommodate at present, not what they could (Schriner & Scotch 2001, second-hand). UALR
  4. F54Difference is neutral: the remedy is changing the interaction between a person and society, and anyone who changes those arrangements can do it (Gill 1994, second-hand). Words like "special services" or "victim of" keep the medical model alive; universal design is named as the framework. UALR
  5. F55Critiques, of the social model rather than of "mismatch": it can treat disabled people's own everyday understanding as partial; the authors prefer starting from people's own perspectives Dewsbury et al. PDF pp. 1–4. It is said to leave out real effects of impairment such as pain and fatigue; a "bio-social" model tries to hold both Bichard & Langdon 2007 summary only. No critique of "mismatch" by name turned up.

The paradigm shift

  1. F56Ability-based design: moves the focus from disability to ability; systems adapt to people instead of demanding abilities they may not have. Wobbrock et al. 2011 summary only
  2. F57Design justice: those most affected by a design have the least say in it. Principles include centring the voices of those directly affected, designers as facilitators, and everyone as an expert on their own experience. Design Justice Network
  3. F58Universal design, inclusive design, design for all: regional names with a shared aim, design for human diversity. "Good design enables, bad design disables" Stockholm Declaration 2004. Critics say universal design still carries "ideologies of ability" (link not recorded). summary only

Critical disability studies

  1. F59Beyond the social model: traditional disability studies centred civil rights and mostly physical disability; critical disability studies widens it to culture, history, power, race and the global South. Stanford Encyclopedia first ~80% read
  2. F60Impairment, three ways: the social model treats impairment as natural and disability as made; critical disability studies (Tremain) says impairment is also shaped by history; others say its real effects get left out (F55). The sources don't agree. Stanford Encyclopedia
  3. F61Ableism as a system: one "normal" body and mind set as the standard (Garland-Thomson's "normate"); a cultural logic of autonomy, self-sufficiency and independence that touches everyone. Goodley 2018 PDF pp. 1–5 Stanford
  4. F62Intersections and authors: intersectional work (feminist, race) turned disability studies into critical disability studies (Meekosha & Shuttleworth 2009, via Goodley). Names in the read sources include Garland-Thomson, Campbell, Tremain, Kafer, McRuer and Goodley. Neither source gives design instructions; they ask to question the norm, include disabled people as authors and link theory to action. Goodley 2018 Stanford

Diversity and variation

  1. F63Neurodiversity paradigm: variation in minds is natural and valuable; one "normal" mind is a cultural fiction; neurodiversity carries the same power dynamics as other diversity. Walker
  2. F64There is no average: of 4,000+ pilots measured on ten body dimensions, almost none were average on all, so cockpits became adjustable. Schools fit an imagined average student; learners have "jagged" profiles. Rose, Harvard Ed.
  3. F65Universal Design for Learning: responds to barriers rooted in biases and systems of exclusion CAST. Planning for learner variability from the start ASCD summary only. The article on UDL changing the concept of disability (Wilson 2017) was blocked.
  4. F66Curb-cut effect: what is built for one group often helps many; target the need and design the full path. Blackwell, SSIR
  5. F67Beyond humans and ages: HCI work on "more-than-human" design moves the human out of the centre TU Delft arXiv 2402.11576 summary only. Nothing found on babies to elderly in the disability sources.

Cognitive and learning design

  1. F68Cognitive accessibility as content design: design, structure and language choices can make content inaccessible. Eight objectives, for example understanding what things are, staying focused, not relying on memory, avoiding and fixing mistakes, getting help and adapting the interface; each with user stories and use/avoid patterns; involve users throughout. W3C COGA first ~20% read
  2. F69A spectrum: people without a diagnosis meet the same difficulties under stress or distraction; for others they last. W3C COGA
  3. F70Dyslexia as a system problem: a 2025 report on young people's school experiences ("Set up for somebody else") asks for classroom standards and assistive technology. British Dyslexia Association news page only. A critic of the dyslexia concept (Elliott) appears in a thesis snippet (link not recorded, summary only).

Do and don'tWhat the sources advise, collected. D1–D10 · N1–N6
  1. D1Name the question briefly, then context, method, outcomes and who they're for (F5).
  2. D2Describe the preferred state and why it is better (F1).
  3. D3Document the process and the reasons for choices so others can follow (F2, F5).
  4. D4Log what changed and why; revisit dropped ideas (F7).
  5. D5Give first-person accounts an interpretive step and a link to wider themes (F17).
  6. D6State study boundaries: time, place, project type, point of view (F19).
  7. D7Treat claims as provisional and open to challenge (F3 summary only, F6).
  8. D8Describe the time limits openly; report when it started, its rhythms and its pauses (F32, F37).
  9. D9Plan at several levels and update the plan; ask "what if" questions early (F40–F42).
  10. D10Give each time box its own deliverable, and rank what can be dropped (F45).
  11. N1Private, unspoken knowing alone doesn't count as research; it has to be shareable (F6).
  12. N2First-person accounts left as narration only (F17).
  13. N3First-person work seen as a shortcut (F20).
  14. N4A How might we challenge used as a research question (F12 summary only; the contrast itself is the helper's reading, not a source's words).
  15. N5A self-set date on its own, with no partial outcomes defined (F45).
  16. N6Expecting a standard length; sources give none (F33, F38).

GlossarySpecial words on this page.
Design thesis
Anna's word for the main claim a design project sets out to explore. No source here uses it as a fixed term.
RtD (research through design)
Research where making things is the way of finding things out.
Preferred state
A description of how things could be better, and why; the made thing shows it.
Practice-based / practice-led
Research built on making. Practice-based: the made thing is the main result. Practice-led: new understanding of the making is the main result.
Constructive design research
Another name for research where building things is central; close to RtD.
Drifting
A design project moving away from its first question, on purpose, towards what the making shows.
Artefact
A made thing in a design project: a prototype, a sketch, a game.
Annotated portfolio
A collection of made things with short notes on what each one taught.
First-person research
Research where the researcher's own experience is the data.
Autoethnography
Writing about your own experience and looking at it the way a researcher looks at a culture.
Autobiographical design
Designing and building something for yourself, using it for real, and learning from that use.
Interpretive step
Going back over an account to say what it means beyond your own case.
How might we
A question form from design thinking ("How might we …?") that turns a found need into a challenge to come up with ideas for.
HCI (human-computer interaction)
The research field about how people use computers.
CHI, DIS, DRS, DiGRA
Research conferences: CHI and DIS (people and computers, design of interactive systems), DRS (Design Research Society), DiGRA (Digital Games Research Association).
AHRB
Arts and Humanities Research Board, a former UK research funder.
Commit
One saved step in a Git repository, with a short message saying what changed.
Playtest
A session where someone plays an unfinished game to see how it works.
PDA
"Persistent drive for autonomy" (also called pathological demand avoidance): a strong need for control over one's own actions; demands, even self-set ones, can block.
Doctoral
About a PhD, the highest university degree, earned through a long research project.
Abstract
The short summary at the top of a research paper.
Search snippet
The few lines a search engine shows under each result.
Second-hand
A page that reports someone else's study instead of being the study itself.
Saved copy
When the web reader showed a PDF as raw code, the helper opened the PDF file the reader had saved on this PC.
Repository
Two meanings here: a university's online archive of papers, or a project folder tracked with Git (a repo).
Preprint, arXiv
A paper shared before other researchers have checked it; arXiv is a free online archive for them.
403
Web error code: the site refused the request.
Milestone
A point in a plan where a named piece of work is finished, often with a date.
Gantt chart
A plan drawn as bars on a timeline, one bar per piece of work.
Buffer, contingency
Extra time kept free in a plan for things that take longer or go wrong.
Time box
A fixed stretch of time with its own small result; when work doesn't fit, less gets done, the time stays.
MoSCoW
A way to rank work: Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time.
Deployment
Putting a made thing into real use for a while, to see what happens.
Field study
A study done in people's real settings instead of a lab.
Prospective memory
Remembering to do something later, such as a planned task.
Mismatch
A gap between what a person needs and how something was designed; it depends on the situation.
Medical model
The view that the problem sits in a person's body or mind, to be fixed or treated.
Social model
The view that barriers in society, not a person's body or mind, block equal participation.
Human rights model
The view that difference is part of human diversity, and that people have a right to support on an equal basis.
Impairment
In the social model: the trait of a person's body or mind, as opposed to the barrier.
Critical disability studies
Research that questions the idea of "normal" itself and looks at power, culture, history and how disability meets race, gender and class.
Ableism
A system that treats one kind of body and mind as the standard and ranks people against it.
Normate
Garland-Thomson's word for the imagined "standard" person that designs and rules are built around.
Intersectional
Looking at how several parts of who someone is (for example disability, race, gender) act together.
Ability-based design
Designing systems that adapt to what people can do, instead of demanding fixed abilities.
Design justice
An approach that gives those most affected by a design the most say in it.
Universal design, inclusive design, design for all
Regional names (US, UK, Europe) for designing for the full range of people.
UDL (Universal Design for Learning)
A framework for planning teaching for learners' differences from the start.
Curb-cut effect
When something built for one group (like a lowered kerb) helps many others too.
COGA
The W3C task force on cognitive and learning accessibility, and its guide.
W3C
World Wide Web Consortium, the group that writes web standards.
Double empathy problem
Milton's idea that misunderstanding between autistic and non-autistic people runs both ways.
More-than-human design
Design research that doesn't put humans alone at the centre; animals, plants and things count too.
Neurodiversity paradigm
Walker's view that variation in minds is natural, that one "normal" mind is a fiction, and that power plays a part.
Workshop proposal
A short paper that invites researchers to a meeting on a topic; it summarises others' work but isn't a study itself.
