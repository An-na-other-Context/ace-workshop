## Decision (Anna (@annaothercrow), 16.11.2026)

### The design/research thesis

Claude's wording, built from Anna's words and her rework, accepted by Anna.

- **Question:** How can I create an accessible, seamless digital game for bottom-up learning in which I and other neurodivergent learners with similar traits learn to build complex technology from scratch?
- **What is learned:** reverse engineering, hardware hacking and building from scratch, for example an IC, a smart device or an operating system.
- **Context:** the places where people learn about technology are designed around one assumed "normal" learner. When a learner's needs don't match that design, the learner gets stuck. That is a design problem, not the learner's fault: a mismatch between needs and design, and between learners and what institutions accommodate today. Where design has failed to accommodate, learners have a right to support on an equal basis.
- **Preferred state:** a learning environment that meets learners where they are and where they need it: a clear outcome, no gaps, nothing assumed down to a set level; a clear puzzle to apply and play with what they learn, with progress they can see; a start with what they have, without buying or knowing everything first; and what they are solving stays in view. So learners gain skills otherwise out of reach for them. (Widened on 18.11.2026 with three findings from Anna's phase 1 sheets; Claude's wording, at Anna's request.)
- **Scope for now:** the game closes the cognitive mismatch first; other mismatches come in later cycles.
- **Made thing:** ACE Workshop, an accessible digital game that stands in for real-world learning places that are uncontrolled, expensive and inaccessible.
- **For whom:** Anna, as designer and learner · other neurodivergent learners with similar traits · the learner profile.
- **Boundaries:** time: a 1-week test cycle, 17.–23.11.2026, then evaluate; the overall goal is done when it's done. Place: Anna's desk with small affordable examples, online forums, makerspaces, PC and repo, inside the game, the design board. Type: research through design + first-person research.
- **Each cycle:** creates and evaluates something experienceable, in a meaningful and measurable way (Anna).

### Research sub-questions

Claude's wording, one per seam of the thesis question, with the context applied; accepted by Anna. They get split further in phase 3, down to questions with clear answers.

1. How can a digital game take a learner from an abstract, unclear top layer down to the layer it is founded on, and back up, so that the top layer makes sense and no knowledge is assumed?
2. How can a digital game guide learners through a layer without written instructions and without them feeling guided, so that the design adapts to the learner, not the other way round?
3. Which cognitive mismatches between the game's design and the needs of Anna and learners with similar traits show up when entering, leaving and returning to a layer?
4. How can a digital game teach learners to find a fault, not only how the parts fit together, without assuming knowledge they may lack?
5. Which skills learned in the digital game carry over to real objects that were out of reach before because of how other learning places are designed?

## What the research shows (AI summary of research.html)

- There is no agreed formula for a "design thesis". Parts that recur: a short question, its context, the method, the outcome and who it's for (F5); a preferred state and why it is better (F1); boundaries of time, place, project type and point of view (F19).
- Questions grow while making and drift; they are often worded late (F7, F8, F10).
- Time: no standard length. First-person studies run from weeks to a decade and mostly end for outside reasons (F33, F35). Planning guides use milestones and a plan that can change; buffer percentages are rules of thumb (F40–F43). A time box helps when its scope can shrink and it has its own small result (F45).
- Reframing: every framing found puts the problem outside the person: in the design, in institutions as they are now, in assumed abilities, in who gets to design, or in the idea of one "normal" (F51–F63). Critics warn that real effects such as pain can drop out of view (F55, F60).
- Nothing was found from ADHD, dyslexic or PDA researchers on wording questions or planning with dates (F14, F29, F48, F73).
- Many sources were seen only as summaries (55 of 92), so several points rest on thin reading.

## Trade-offs accepted
