In short

Less, one at a time.The read sources agree: one step or question at a time, about five choices at most, minimal design, clear headings, a short main path (F1, F17, F20, F21, F25).
Icons beside words, not more icons.Nothing found says more icons, colour or emphasis help. COGA: a familiar icon next to its text, not too many. Autistic students preferred text over visuals (F1, F19). This pulls against the learner's "more symbols" trial rule; see the tension box.
Say what it's for, and how big.Open with the purpose; give each step its why and rough size (F2, F15, F23).
The setting adjusts, not the reader.Neurodivergent authors: let people set density and detail; skip and pause must keep you included (F8, F9, F10).
Demand pressure: untested.No study tests action bars, badges or counters with PDA readers. PDA guidance favours choice, possibility lists, fewer demands; declarative wording helps some and burdens others (F35–F38).

Thin direct evidence: one co-design study with autistic students was read in part; most hierarchy advice comes from guidelines, not from tests with these readers (F7).

What it could mean, by place

My reading of the findings, not decided. Numbers point to the findings below.

Cards

  • One step, purpose on top (F15, F25)
  • Why + rough size in one line (F2, F23)
  • ≤ 5 choices; extras behind a labelled fold (F20, F26)
  • Icons only beside words (F19)
  • Skip and Pause as real options (F9)

Pages

  • Key information first; headings that chunk (F29, F15)
  • Left-aligned, line 50–75 characters, spacing 1.5 (F29)
  • Fold only what some readers need; say what's inside (F26)
  • Few statuses; done items plain (F27)
  • No italics, underline or all caps for emphasis (F28)

Chat replies

  • Answer or action first (F34)
  • Headings and steps, not bullets everywhere (F31, F33)
  • Fewer hedges; brief, then the action (F31)
  • Offer a format choice (F33)
Tension with the trial rules from 20.11 (mine)
  • "More symbols and graphics" (the learner's test-card note) vs F1, F19: text first, icons beside words, few. Could fit as icons that label, never decorate. The learner's own preference counts, as with dyslexia fonts (F30).
  • Do bar "Do: ① tap ② write ③ Send" is imperative; F35, F36 point to choice and possibility wording. Untested either way (E1).

Q1 · Similar traitsWhat research with autistic, ADHD and dyslexic people shows. F1–F7
  1. F1Autistic students want minimal design, text over visuals, logical hierarchy, clear navigation, no motion or sound, and a way to personalise. Images only where words can't show it. 280 survey answers, 16 case studies, 3 co-design workshops. StudyBarrier: busy, decorative or moving layouts the reader has to filter. Change: text first, decoration only if it carries meaning, a personalise control.
  2. F2Unclear "why" and "how much time" block starting a task. Same study. StudyBarrier: a step shown without purpose or size. Change: one line with the why and a rough size.
  3. F3Complex interfaces hurt performance and choice-making; how a control links to the task must be explicit. Earlier studies (Grynszpan 2008, Biju 2013), second-hand via the same paper. StudyBarrier: buttons whose effect isn't stated. Change: each button says what it does.
  4. F4For ADHD adults, task tools assume steady self-regulation and linear time; nonlinear attention should be accommodated. 22 interviews, 20 people in concept sessions. Study summary onlyBarrier: one fixed order. Change: flexible order, skip and come back.
  5. F5Dyslexic readers: font size mattered more than line spacing; larger text and wider letter spacing sped reading. Rello and colleagues, 2013 and 2017. Study summary onlyBarrier: small, tight text. Change: size and spacing before other aids.
  6. F6Dyslexic web searchers preferred headings and bullets over long blocks. Cited in the LaMPost paper. Study summary only
  7. F7Gap: no study found on size and weight, icons with or without words, density or folded sections with ADHD, autistic or PDA adults. The helper's searches of ACM conference work (SIGACCESS / ASSETS, CHI) turned up none of these.
Q2 · Affirming and co-designNeurodiversity-affirming design, critical disability studies, co-design and participatory design. F8–F14
  1. F8Disability as friction between a trait and a setting; access works when the setting adapts. A neurodivergent author's lived-experience paper, ASSETS 2024 (first part read). LivedBarrier: fixed demands of the tool. Change: the tool adapts.
  2. F9Being offered to skip isn't access unless another way keeps you included. Routine apps felt too rigid; tools should follow daily energy; clutter calls for adjustable sensory settings. Same paper; no concrete layout rules. LivedBarrier: token skip, fixed density. Change: skip or pause that keeps you in; reader-set density.
  3. F10Most ADHD technology is built to "mitigate" behaviour by neurotypical standards, and rarely with ADHD people. Neurodivergent researchers, CHI 2022. Review summary onlyBarrier: success measured as conforming. Change: judge by whether the reader can do it their way.
  4. F1184 social-computing papers, mostly medical framing; the review argues for grounding in critical disability theory. Review summary only
  5. F12Co-design: offer activities as optional, open invitations; treat participants as experts; a deficit focus is itself a barrier. Martin 2015, second-hand via the autistic-students paper. Study
  6. F13Co-design and participatory design work exists but wasn't read: ADHD children and carers (CHI 2023), a review of design with autistic adults (2023), and a scoping review where most studies lacked neurodivergent input and personalisation kept coming up. Study Review Review summary only
  7. F14An audit of AI replies counts conformity as no success, and checks that replies don't push users to mask. Preprint, no human readers. Study
Q3 · COGAW3C's guide to cognitive accessibility: patterns for hierarchy and flow. About two thirds read. F15–F24

Read through the web reader's summaries in three parts; the last third (the end of 4.8 and 4.9, adaptation) wasn't read in detail. Source for all: Guideline

  1. F15Make the purpose clear (4.2.1) and the structure clear (4.3.3), so people can find their place again after a distraction.Barrier: a panel that doesn't say what it is. Change: open with its purpose.
  2. F16Show where you are (4.2.4): done, current, still to come. Known from the overview table only.
  3. F17Separate each instruction (4.4.9): one step each, even obvious ones; numbered lists; if-then tables for conditions.
  4. F18Make important actions easy to find (4.3.4), visible without scrolling or hunting.
  5. F19Icons (4.2.7, 4.2.5): a familiar icon beside the content it describes, one meaning each, not too many; controls named in words.Barrier: icons that stand alone or decorate. Change: icon + word, few icons.
  6. F20Avoid too much content (4.6.3): about five main choices per screen; extras behind a clear "more".
  7. F21Keep the critical path short (4.6.2): only the steps needed; optional ones moved out of the main path.
  8. F22Limit interruptions (4.6.1): no pop-ups, alerts or moving content unless the person starts them; offer a pause.
  9. F23Help people prepare (4.6.4): time needed, what's needed, an overview of steps, and clear "in progress" and "done".
  10. F24Not relying on memory (4.7), help (4.8), going back and undo (4.5), adapting the interface (4.9). Names and aims from the overview table only. COGA doesn't cover demand wording; "avoid time pressure" (4.5.9) is closest.
Q4 · Design systemsGOV.UK and the Home Office posters: patterns in daily use. F25–F28
  1. F25Start with one question per page; group questions only when research shows it helps. No study cited on the page. ExampleBarrier: several asks on one screen. Change: one ask per card.
  2. F26Fold only what some people need; never hide what most need. Some people avoid the fold link, thinking it leaves the page. General users, not neurodivergent-specific. ExampleBarrier: unclear what a fold does. Change: the fold label says what's inside.
  3. F27Task lists: few statuses (each one more to remember); done items plain so open ones stand out; red only for errors. Example
  4. F28Home Office posters. Autism: simple, consistent layouts, plain words, bullets, no walls of text, buttons that say what they do. Dyslexia: left-align, no italics, underline or all caps, not too much in one place, remind people instead of making them remember, let them change text and background. Advice can conflict, so test. No ADHD poster. Guideline
Q5 · Dyslexia guidesLayout values for text. F29–F30
  1. F29Dyslexia Scotland's values: letter and word spacing +30–35%; line spacing 1.5; lines 50–75 characters; left-aligned, not justified; headings about 1.5× per level; sentences up to 25 words; paragraphs up to 5 lines; key information first; tinted background; contrast at least 4.5:1. GuidelineBarrier: dense, justified, tight text. Change: these values on pages.
  2. F30Specialist dyslexia fonts show no consistent benefit in research; use one if the reader likes it. Same guide. The learner's choice of Dyslexic Close stands on this. Guideline

Q6 · Chat repliesFormatting text answers. F31–F34
  1. F31An audit of 576 AI replies: told about neurodivergent readers, replies got 22% longer, with more headings and finer steps; bullet density stayed the same; hedging fell by over 75%; about one emoji per reply. Rules used: headings or numbered steps instead of long paragraphs; start with the easiest step; brief acknowledgement, then the action. Not tested with readers. StudyBarrier: dense paragraphs. Change: structure by headings and steps, not by more bullets.
  2. F32People write their own custom prompts because default AI replies are dense and neurotypical. A 2025 analysis of 61 Reddit groups, second-hand via the audit. Study
  3. F33Complaints about over-bulleted replies; advice to offer a choice of format (bullets, table, prose). Opinion Opinion summary only
  4. F34Lead with the answer or action: key information first (F29), important actions easy to find (F18). No test with chat.
Q7 · Demand pressureCan "what to do" emphasis add pressure? F35–F38
  1. F35Direct commands can trigger avoidance because they feel like no choice; observations leave room. Caveats: some people prefer commands, since declarative wording asks them to work out the answer; make room for "no"; "let's" spreads the demand. Written for parents. GuidelineBarrier: each step framed as an order. Change: say what the step is; offer, don't command.
  2. F36For adults: "possibility" lists instead of rigid to-dos, a real option not to, fewer demands per day. Not a study; notes PDA is contested. Guideline
  3. F37Adult accounts: your own to-dos can trigger avoidance; notifications and deadlines feel like a constant low threat. Lived Opinion Opinion Guideline summary only
  4. F38Gap: no study tests action bars, red badges or counters with PDA readers. That they add pressure is plausible, not shown.

GlossarySpecial words on this page.
Visual hierarchy
What the eye sees first, second and third, set by size, weight, position and spacing.
Flow
The order in which you read and do things on a card, page or reply.
Barrier in the design
What a reader has to make up for themselves because the design doesn't do it (as on a page from a private project).
COGA
Cognitive and Learning Disabilities Accessibility: a W3C guide on making content usable for people with cognitive and learning differences.
W3C
The group that writes web standards.
GOV.UK Design System
The UK government's shared set of tested page parts and patterns.
Home Office posters
Do-and-don't posters from a UK government department, one per group of users.
SIGACCESS, ASSETS
The computing society's accessibility group and its yearly research conference.
CHI, CSCW
Large research conferences on how people use computers, and on computers in group and social work.
Neurodiversity-affirming
Treating different kinds of minds as natural variation, not as faults to fix.
Critical disability studies
Research that questions the idea of one "normal" body and mind, and who sets it.
Co-design, participatory design
Designing together with the people who will use the thing, as experts on their own experience.
Masking
Hiding your own ways of thinking or acting to fit in.
PDA
Pathological demand avoidance, or persistent drive for autonomy: demands, even wanted ones, can trigger avoidance.
Declarative wording
Describing ("the bins go out tonight") instead of instructing ("take the bins out").
Fold
A section that opens and closes; also called details or progressive disclosure.
Critical path
The steps you can't skip to finish a task.
Audit (of AI replies)
Measuring many replies from AI models, without people reading them.
Hedging
Softening words like "might", "perhaps", "it seems".
Contrast 4.5:1
How much darker the text is than its background; 4.5 to 1 is the usual minimum for body text.
Abstract
The short summary at the top of a research paper.
Search snippet
The few lines a search engine shows under each result.
Second-hand
A page that reports someone else's study instead of being the study itself.
Saved copy
When the web reader showed a PDF as raw code, the helper opened the PDF file the reader had saved on this PC.
Repository
A university's online archive of papers.
Preprint, arXiv
A paper shared before other researchers have checked it; arXiv is a free online archive for them.
403, 404
Web error codes: 403 = the site refused the request, 404 = the page wasn't found.
